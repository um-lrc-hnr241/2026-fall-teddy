Build 1

Build 1: Amelia

Amelia Koenig

Honors 241

September 2026

Draft 1 Build - Teddys

If our attention and behavior can be directed by things outside our conscious control, how much agency does consciousness really have? We often think of consciousness as the part of ourselves responsible for controlling what we pay attention to, what we think about, and what we do. If a person consciously thinks about something, it seems natural to assume they would direct their own thoughts and behavior. Alfred Gell’s discussion of decorative objects and patterns in Art and Agency: Anthropological Theory, however, complicates this relationship.

Gell attributes a surprising amount of power to an object over the person observing it. He describes patterns as objects that attract people, hold their attention, influence their decisions, and therefore create relationships that continue over time. Through Gell’s descriptions of the ways decorative objects have the capacity to attract and influence people, he suggests that consciousness is not simply an independent force that directs our interactions with the world. Instead, consciousness emerges through an ongoing relationship between our minds and the things in the world around us.

Gell first establishes this relationship by arguing decoration can actively influence human behavior. He writes, “decorative patterns applied to artifacts attach people to things, and to the social projects those things entail” (Gell 74). His example, however, is surprisingly simple. A child might be more willing to go to bed if the bed has attractive sheets decorated with spaceships, dinosaurs, or even polka dots. The decoration does not physically force the child into bed, and the child doesn't consciously reason because these sheets have patterns I like, so I have decided to go to sleep. Nevertheless, the decoration may shape the child’s behavior if he associates the pattern with good sleep and rest. This example complicates the idea that conscious decisions are always the direct source of our actions. The child going to sleep may experience the bed as something they are choosing, but Gell points towards other forces that shape that decision.

Throughout his writing, Gell argues that consciousness does not necessarily make the observer perform an obvious movement. Instead, it notices an object or design, interprets it, remembers, and then returns with an action. In a way, Gell suggests that consciousness may function less like a commander and more like a processor of environmental cues that it can never perfectly master. What our conscious minds do depends on what the world outside gives it to process.

An object does not merely sit passively, it participates in producing and shaping a person’s response. A person’s consciousness may still participate, but its contribution is a response to influences that it did not create. Gell develops his ideas further through his concept of complex patterns as “unfinished business”. He argues that because complicated patterns resist complete understanding through visual inspection, they remain cognitively incomplete. According to Gell, “the process can continue interminably; the pattern is inexhaustible” (Gell 80). It is almost as if the observer cannot fully control their own attention. A person cannot just glance at the object, completely understand it, and move on. We are drawn to an object's complexities, and in turn it dictates and sustains our thoughts and actions. The conscious mind attempts to understand the pattern, but its complexity prevents the observer from easily reaching a final understanding. Even though the observer is consciously thinking about the pattern, the decision to continue examining it is shaped by the pattern itself. Conscious thought is directed by something outside of consciousness.

A person may consciously examine a pattern, but the pattern itself determines which parts are there for examination and why the mind continues returning to it. Gell’s writing offers an unusual perspective over whether consciousness is the main influence over our actions. He does not argue that consciousness is powerless; the observer is clearly thinking, perceiving, and attempting to understand. After all, the observer still interprets and responds to the object rather than simply obeying it, suggesting that outside influence does not completely eliminate conscious choice. Gell seems to locate a person's agency somewhere between the object and the person, rather than entirely in the conscious mind. Not only is the consciousness active, but it is also being acted upon. In Gell’s interpretation, consciousness partially participates in perception and action, but it does so in constant interaction with the world that acts upon it.
