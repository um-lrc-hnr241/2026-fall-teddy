Build 3

Build 3: Rebecca

When I first encountered Gell’s Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, I understood the relationships between objects and living things as relatively uncomplicated: objects acquire agency through their relationships with people, and in turn affect the outside world. When studying the reading more, this analysis seems to be incomplete. Gell's argument is not only that objects have their own autonomy, but that their influence is continuous, stretching across past, present, and future. An object's effect does not end when the original action ends; it persists, and can change in meaning as the people around it change. This realization bestows greater power on the material world. Fundamentally, Gell's work can be seen through the lens of reconsolidation. Just as a memory is altered each time it is recalled, identity according to Gell is never fixed, and its revision does not take place entirely in a person's mind. Through reconsolidation, the past is carried into the present, where it can be reshaped and go on to affect the future. Identities develop through evolving relationships with the material world, as objects change in meaning alongside the people who interact with them.

Gell builds this point through the idea of unfinished business. He describes the relationship between a carpet and owner as “inexhaustible” (80): this connection is never fully resolved but continuous throughout the owner's life. The key to this continuity is that the exchange between the living and the material world is never equal. There always seems to be a sort of “delay” or “lag” (81) between said transactions, meaning the interaction is never truly closed. This lag links Gell's argument to reconsolidation. Over time, the object becomes part of how the owner understands themselves and how others understand them, creating a feedback loop in which it becomes difficult to separate the person's developing identity from the object. Identity, in this sense, is not stored privately and then expressed outward; it is repeatedly revised through contact with things.

Gell's concept of the distributed person extends this point from a single object to a person’s whole surrounding. He highlights the difficulty of distinguishing between what “people do as persons, and what people do as things” (124). Where most of us would easily separate a person from an object, Gell suggests that, on a closer examination, it becomes hard to tell whether an action stems from a person's own consciousness or from some outside influence. The distributed person argues that a person's existence is not confined strictly to their body, but instead made up of various networks from their surroundings including relationships, objects, and circumstances. One might object that the carpet itself never changes and only the owner’s mind does. But Gell’s point is that the two can not be cleanly separated. Therefore, a person's identity will never be stable; it is constantly being modified and transformed as a result of the outside world.

Gell's discussion of apotropaic art reveals the mechanism behind this continuity. Apotropaic objects are designed to ward off evil, and Gell describes them as protecting an agent from a hostile recipient. Many do so by captivating this evil and expelling it. A malevolent spirit becomes absorbed in trying to escape the difficult pattern and work out the design, making it unable to pass. The pattern protects by creating unfinished business for the very thing it guards against. The same lag that keeps the owner bound to the carpet becomes a trap, since the demon, like the owner, can never fully exhaust the pattern. Although Gell writes about spirits, I would suggest that this mechanism may persist today in a different form. Specific stimuli exist as a way to ward off which can be defined as “inner demons.” This could be a decorated surface, a story line, or even technology. In both modern day and past interpretations, patterns protect by creating unfinished business for the very thing it guards against. The actual action of the artist creating the art is finished, yet the object’s protective work is not; it continues long after its creation, every time a threat is present. This threat could be seen as an outer demon, or as mentioned, an inner demon inside our mind. The protective object carries the past with it, created due to a prior need to ward against evil. This past continues to shape present and future encounters. The object functions almost like an externalized memory: it preserves an early concern and reactivates it in new circumstances. The past is not simply remembered, but continually put back into use, shaping the identity of those it protects.

The labyrinth shows what this entanglement looks like from the inside, when the mind caught in the pattern is a human one. The Cretan labyrinth evokes a navigational problem, without actually offering one: it creates a cognitive obstacle even though there is a way through. Yet where the demon remains trapped, the human viewer can work through the pattern by grasping the “iterative rule in connecting up lines and points” (88). What matters here is not only that the viewer is eventually able to untangle the maze, but that their understanding is rebuilt in stages through repeated engagement. The confusion draws them in, and the search leads them to uncover the structure beneath. Each return to the pattern revises what the viewer previously believed about it, much as each recall of a memory revises the memory, showing how consciousness continually reorganizes its understanding through encounters with the material world.

Taken together, these examples push Gell’s account of objects beyond the idea of a single interaction. Through the lens of reconsolidation, the relationship between people and objects becomes a continuous process in which the past shapes what comes next, and objects take on a greater role than I first recognized. This in turn raises the question of the power of living things themselves. Unfinished business says that something from the past has the ability to continue to matter in the present, even when the original agent is no longer acting. Actions may be finished, but effects are not. Gell's example asks how consciousness responds to effects that originate outside itself, and whether the revision of the self is something we carry alone, or something we undertake together with the objects around us.
