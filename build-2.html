Build 2

Build 2: Group build

At the start of every new loop in the Westworld Park, a single tin can falls off the back of a horse belonging to Dolores Abernathy. No matter who picks it up—Dolores, a fellow host, or guest—the single action of picking up a tin will set in motion an entire new storyline for the park. Who would expect that a single tin can could completely alter the trajectory of both a guest’s time within the park, and the experience of the host? There are many objects throughout the Westworld park curated by Ford and Arnold, which are the key to helping both the guests and hosts find their own consciousness. Alfred Gell offers the same perspective when it comes to objects, highlighting how objects all around us are key to curating our own personhood and idea of self consciousness. Whether it's a host, a symbol, or just a regular tin can, Alfred Gell’s discussion in Art and Agency: Anthropological Theory questions the idea in Westworld that consciousness independently controls our thoughts and actions.

Gell suggests that conscious decisions emerge through interactions with forces in the environment. Although the hosts appear to make conscious decisions, their actions are also shaped by programmed narratives, memories, and external triggers. Similarly, the guests may think they are making their own choices, but they are really following the manufactured storylines of Westworld. Gell’s argument presents viewers with a new way to understand the show’s uncertainty about whether the hosts are truly conscious. Rather than asking whether the hosts have complete control over their actions, Gell’s ideas encourage us to ask whether consciousness requires complete independence at all. If human behavior is also constantly influenced by our surroundings, the distinction between human and host consciousness becomes less clear.

Further, Gell conceptualizes a voluntary action as requiring an interaction between one and the social context–the network of other people, relationships, and situational cues that an agent responds to and is shaped by–or else it would be involuntary. One’s consciousness stems from those interactions; So, hosts like Bernard are mistaken for humans because of their lifelikeness. As they react to their surroundings and act accordingly, they reflect the social environment Gell mentions. However, Ford and others strongly affirm that the hosts are not real as they are and cannot feel emotions regarding their surroundings, like embarrassment. They simply are coded to act a certain way, except that argument breaks when they go rogue and when they are reminded of previous storylines. ​

We also find Gell's claim that personhood is “spread” across memories, relationships, and objects (Gell 104) reframing the hosts of Westworld not as machines awaiting consciousness, but as beings already made up by traces of their creator. Looking towards Westworld, the hosts can be seen as decorated objects, their decorations serve as a backstory, memories, and physical features. This makes the hosts’ loyalty and loops an extension of their maker's agency rather than as evidence of their own. This is the same problem Gell leaves unresolved in art. Agency seems to emerge from relationships between the creator, object and viewer, not from any of them alone. We see the show dramatize this through the memories implemented, edited, and partially retained across resets.

In one example, Alfred Gell illustrates how a lime container acquires agency after affecting the perception of those who interact with it. This demonstrates that relationships built between objects and living things grant objects a sense of agency, even when the objects are not conscious themselves. In the start of season one, hosts are established as non-conscious like a decorative object, however, throughout the show these decorations allow them to create relationships with guests, proving that decorated objects can produce social and emotional effects. Only later does the idea of something beyond decoration within the hosts become possible, testing the distinction between decorated function and consciousness.

Gell’s argument of object agency is furthered when it's seen completely mirrored in Westworld's use of the “maze” symbol. Coming from a basic object, a kids toy, the symbol of the maze creates a deeper meaning for multiple characters. The man in black obsesses with this external idea, sure of the fact that it will uncover a deeper meaning in his life. Dolores also follows the voice in her head to the center of “the maze,” which when she reaches it, she finds her own consciousness. For both of these characters, the greater meaning of a conscious and full personhood is found in these external forces caused by a simple object. Gell is making this exact same argument. Equally importantly, the use of the maze is a method of “captivation”, as Gell would describe. The experience of being captivated by this object for these characters gives them their conscious will. While Arnold focuses on reprogramming the internal self to create consciousness, maybe it was really an outside object that led to real consciousness.

As opposed to “the maze” symbol in Westworld, Gell uses the example of Celtic knotwork to raise a question about the relationship between consciousness and control. The evil spirit has its own “malevolent plan,” but becomes so fascinated by the pattern that it loses interest in what it originally intended to do. For the Man in Black, although he believes that searching for the Maze is his own choice, his fascination with it increasingly determines where he goes and what he does. Like Gell’s demon, the Man in Black is not physically forced to follow the pattern, yet he struggles to pull his attention away from it. This comparison makes us question whether being conscious of our intentions actually means we control them, especially when our attention can be shaped by something outside ourselves.
