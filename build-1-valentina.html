Build 1: Valentina

Valentina Silveira Hianke

Honors 241

9/18/26

Build 1

It is often imagined that consciousness is a voice trapped inside the walls of our brain, but what if it extends beyond the body? Alfred Gell’s discussion in Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory reframes this question by challenging the distinction between the “inner” and “outer” self as only relative. He argues that a person is not confined to a particular body but can extend through relationships, memories, objects, and material traces (Gell 104). This argument becomes significant when considering whether consciousness is solely responsible for human agency, or whether agency is spread across a much larger network.

Gell begins by discussing Daniel Dennett’s criticism of the homunculus, the supposed “little person” inside the brain who perceives the world and makes decisions. This metaphor creates a problem: if an observer sits inside the brain, who observes the observer? And who controls the controller? (Hoffman). Gell’s discussion opposes the idea of consciousness as a hidden person sitting inside us. Rather than searching for the “real” person inside the brain, he encourages us to consider the person as relationships with the world. The “person” is less like a single point and more like a web, extending beyond the physical body—a challenge to Gell’s concept of distributed personhood or the assumption of a single internal source responsible for perception, decision-making and thoughts. If there is no “little person” making decisions, then consciousness can not be simply understood as an internal observer directing behavior.

Instead, Gell writes that a person and their mind are not confined to particular “spatio-temporal coordinates,” but consist of a “spread” of biographical events, memories, and objects (Gell 104). The word “spread” is significant because it suggests that personhood has no fixed location. A person creates their traces through their possessions, memories, relationships, and creativity, which continue to exist even after the individual is no longer physically present. However, this raises a question of whether consciousness has causal power. If physical processes in the brain can explain behavior, then consciousness might appear almost like a passenger, experiencing what happens without influencing it—though a passenger still experiences and interprets the journey, so the metaphor does not make consciousness meaningless. Gell’s argument does not solve this problem, but complicates how it is framed. If the person is distributed beyond the brain, then perhaps agency cannot be understood simply by looking for a single internal source.

Art makes this especially visible. A painting can carry traces of an artist’s intentions into the future, remaining long after its creator has died, continuing to influence viewers. The artist does not need to remain present for their agency to remain active through the artwork. The artwork becomes part of a larger web of agency, carrying something of the person into places and times where they cannot go (Gell 221-223). This is not unique to art; Gell does not argue that objects are conscious in the same way humans are. Instead, objects can carry an extension of human agency through memories, and emotional reactions (Gell 76). A portrait, for instance, might preserve the presence of someone who is no longer alive; a personal object might evoke memories and behavior. In each scenario, something outside the body participates in the continuing mosaic of a person—agency may belong more broadly to the network of people and objects surrounding it, while consciousness remains one part of that process.

Still, Gell’s argument leaves open where this agency actually belongs. If agency belongs to the artist, how do their intentions remain after the artist is gone? If agency belongs to the object itself, this is difficult to explain as an object does not have intentions or consciousness of its own. Perhaps, then, if agency emerges through the artist, the work, and the viewer together, it does not need one specific location. Instead, agency may emerge through these connections. This fits Gell’s theory that a person is composed of different actions, responses and intentions, making agency distributed rather than located in one place.

Gell changes the way we view consciousness but does not resolve the debate. Instead, his idea challenges the boundary the debate depends on. His image of a web of traces replaces the idea of consciousness being a sealed room and art makes this vivid: intentions leave the mind and continue affecting the world through objects decades later. Whether this means consciousness itself is causally powerful remains unresolved. Gell suggests that understanding human agency may require us to explore beyond the brain and toward the network of people, objects, memories, and relationships through which a person continues to exist and act upon.
