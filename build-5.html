Build 5

Rebecca

Both Scarry and Gell develop the idea of reconsolidation through the projection of human awareness onto objects, and through a relationship between person and object that extends beyond their first interaction. For Scarry, the impulse to throw a broken chair against a wall does not come from that moment alone. The person is “continuing to act out of the context of the normal situation…rather than out of the immediate moment” (296), drawing on a long history. Similarly, Gell talks about apotropaic art, which brings in a purpose from the past. The kolam-based tattoo serves as both a “map” (90), guiding the dead women to her kin, and a puzzle for those who meet the pattern as strangers. Likewise, Scarry's aspirin bottle, which contains “within its design a test” (305), depends on the mind who encounters it. Both authors describe reconciliation as a process not confined to the mind, but shared between people and the objects that hold their histories.

While both authors describe objects coming back to change people, they disagree on whether that change finishes. Scary describes projection as a “form of revision in which the original given is utterly eliminated and replaced by something wholly other than itself” (285). This revision is driven by a specific need. She describes pain as "something which cannot be felt without being wished unfelt,” and the chair's shape as “the shape of perceived pain wished gone” (290). The chair serves the purpose of solving a problem, the weight of the body, and once that problem is solved, it no longer holds much of a purpose. The chair then settles into a new norm, seen through a human acting out of “the context of the normal situation” (296). Gell's revision, by contrast, is an unfinished loop that can “continue interminably” (80). Rather than settling it as a norm, the carpet’s owner keeps finding new meaning in the pattern, as the eye “singles out now this relation, this symmetry, now that” (80). Each return reopens the relationship causing the viewer to discover something new. Therefore, while Scarry's revision seems to reach an endpoint, Gell’s revision remains open to new influences.

Moreover, Scarry and Gell diverge when it comes to identifying whose past an object carries. Gell focuses on the individual, describing the relationship between carpet and owner as "inexhaustible" (80). Scarry expands this to the past of a civilization. She explains how an aspirin bottle holds “the projected knowingness of its collective makers” (305), focusing not just on an individual maker, but instead a larger group. These two differing points illustrate how reconsolidation can be seen in various forms. It can occur at the scale of a culture, as well as a single person. This difference in turn shapes how people experience objects. For Gell, revision depends on encounters. The carpet means something to its owner that no one else could share. In Scarry's account, a person can benefit from a history they never lived. Someone taking an aspirin is exposed to generations of knowledge without ever knowing it, as the object meets them already prepared. Gell's objects are experienced through discovery, while Scarry’s are experienced through inheritance.

As Scarry and Gell produce different interpretations to reconsolidation, they also come to different conclusions on what happens when an object meets something with which it holds no past. For Gell, the pattern overrides it. The demon trapped by apotropaic suffers a “paralysis of the will” (84), losing the intention it arrived with and gaining nothing in its place. This can be seen in Gell's description of patterns as “potential weapons in situations of conflict” (83). Scarry, by contrast, shows how the absence of history can prove beneficial. A poem can “remake human sentience" and “[alter] the alive percipience of other persons” (307). This result is equally as possible with an absence of history. A person will leave the interaction changed for the better, because the object carries the poet’s awareness to them. Gell's patterns test minds to defeat them, while Scarry’s objects test minds to care for them. Objects not only measure what a mind brings to them, but can also supply a past that a stranger never lived.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that objects preserve perceptions, not just memories, and that reconsolidation therefore takes place between minds, not just within them. For Scarry, an artifact “cannot be sentiently aware of pain,” yet its design “is the structure of a perception” (289). For Gell, the “projection, or externalization, of the agency involved in perception…into the thing perceived” is “the source of its animation” (78). When someone makes an object, their understanding of the world gets built into it and lasts long after they are gone. When another person later encounters that object, they take up the maker's understanding and reshape with their own. This shared insight can help us better understand why the authors approach reconsolidation so differently. If an object carries its maker's perception, does revision end once that perception has accomplished its goal, or does it continue as new minds meet it? The answer can depend on its purpose: an object built to end a need, such as pain, can finish its work, while an object built to sustain a relationship never can. This suggests objects have the ability to link one's mind's past to another mind's present. Reconsolidation, then, is a social process in which minds revise one another through the things they make, which shows how consciousness can act on the physical world long after its maker has stopped acting.

Both Gell and Scarry use reconsolidation in order to illustrate how objects hold the past open, and whether that past is revised or left fixed depends on what the mind brings to it. Yet, how can reconsolidation allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? In a purely physical chain, the same cause should produce the same effect. However, Gell's tattoo example shows how a design can both lead a dead woman to her kin, while also trapping an evil demon, even though its lines are identical for both. This diversion depends on the history brought to the interaction. Because reconsolidation allows that history to be revised, future effects are not fixed in advance. And since both authors place memory in objects as well as minds, this revision cannot be reduced to brain operations alone. Through the things it makes, consciousness projects its awareness into the physical world, where it continues to redirect other minds long after the interaction has ended.

Build 5: Scarry and Gell

Alfred Gell and Elaine Scarry offer complementary ways of understanding narration as the coordination of successive states over time into a meaningful sequence in which each moment contributes to a larger purpose. Their approaches raise the question of whether the continuation of an intention also means the continuation of the consciousness that produced it. For Gell, an artist’s intention becomes embedded in an artwork, allowing it to influence people through their interactions with the object. Similarly, Scarry examines how imagination and design organize individual elements around an anticipated outcome. Comparing their approaches reveals that narration is not simply the organization of events into a coherent sequence, but a process through which intention gives direction to what happens beyond its original moment. While Gell emphasizes how an intention produces effects through an object and its viewers, Scarry highlights how an anticipated outcome shapes the arrangement of individual parts. Together, their approaches suggest that consciousness can influence events through intentions given external form, even after the original conscious act has ended.

Although Gell and Scarry share this understanding of narration, they differ in how they explain the relationship between an original intention and the events that follow. On page 80, Gell explores how intricate patterns establish ongoing relationships between an object and its viewers through their inability to fully understand its organization. Viewers can recognize individual shapes and repetitions without understanding how all the elements relate. This difficulty produces what Gell calls a “pleasurable frustration,” as viewers become mentally absorbed in the artwork without reaching a complete understanding (Gell 80). He describes the pattern as a “mind-trap” because it continually draws viewers back into interpretation (Gell 80). Gell further explains that patterns present the mind with “unfinished business,” as viewers continue discovering new relationships and symmetries within the design (Gell 80). This suggests that the artist’s original intention remains influential through the artwork, even as viewers develop interpretations beyond what the artist intended. The artwork carries the effects of the artist’s decisions, but those effects can develop beyond the artist’s control.

Scarry, on the other hand, approaches narration through the ability of human imagination to transform internal experiences into external objects that respond to human needs. In pages 288–290, she describes the natural world as indifferent to human suffering, explaining that it is “immune, inanimate, inhuman” and does not recognize human vulnerability (Scarry 288). Through imagination, humans reconstruct the external world as though it could understand and respond to their experiences. Scarry illustrates this through the example of a tree during a flood, imagining how a mythmaker might give it a compassionate voice that warns someone of rising waters and offers its branches for protection. Although the tree cannot literally speak or feel compassion, transforming it into a raft or boat allows it to provide the protection its imagined voice promises (Scarry 289). Scarry further develops this idea through the example of a chair, explaining that its shape is not simply a representation of the human body but rather the shape of “perceived-pain-wished-gone” (Scarry 290). This wording emphasizes that the chair originates in the recognition of discomfort and the desire to eliminate it. Rather than imitating the body, its design transforms the wish for relief into something physical. Unlike Gell, who emphasizes how an object generates unpredictable relationships through interpretation, Scarry emphasizes how an imagined purpose becomes embodied in an object. The chair does not understand human discomfort, but its design allows it to fulfill a purpose that originated in a human experience. The person who imagines the object does not need to remain present for it to continue serving that purpose. Still, an object's purpose may change as others use it in ways its creator never anticipated.

Despite their different approaches, Gell and Scarry ultimately reveal how narration allows an original intention to continue shaping the world beyond the moment in which it was formed. Gell demonstrates this through artworks that create ongoing relationships with viewers, whose interpretations can develop beyond the artist’s control. Scarry, meanwhile, shows how imagination transforms human experiences and desires into objects that continue serving the purposes for which they were created. Although Gell emphasizes the unpredictability of an object's influence while Scarry emphasizes the continuation of its intended purpose, both show how an internal intention can take external form and remain influential beyond its creator's presence. Together, they show that an intention can remain influential even when its outcomes exceed its creator's expectations. Reading them together reinforces the distinction between the continuation of an intention and the continuation of the consciousness that produced it. An object does not preserve the consciousness behind its creation, but it carries forward something that consciousness established: a direction, purpose, or possibility that shapes what happens next. Ultimately, narration connects these two processes by showing how consciousness gives form to intentions that continue developing through the world and the people who encounter them. What persists is not consciousness itself, but its influence, which continues to shape events and take on new meanings long after the original conscious act has ended.

Alfred Gell and Elaine Scarry both approach phenomenality from an unconventional direction. Instead of looking inside the head, they both study how inanimate objects carry and affect the structure of felt experience. Gell describes this as the “objectification of personhood” (74) and Scarry describes it by saying objects are meant “to assist, amplify, or alter the felt-experience of sentience rather than merely to populate the external world with shapes and mechanisms” (283). Both of their arguments share the same three-step process. To start, they both emphasize that an object’s form can only be explained from the experience of the person who makes or encounters it. Secondly, they both believe that constant interactions with these objects provide a reciprocation of experience that helps further our sense of self. They then focus on an integrated single whole felt experience that these objects hold together.

Despite these shared steps, Scarry and Gell diverge over which experience objects hold together. For Gell, the experience is captivation and animation. As we gaze at complex patterns and decorations, we constantly try to understand their parts but can never fully grasp their true form. To see a pattern at all, the eye has to move from motif to motif and register that each matches its neighbor, and Gell argues that the agency of this perceptual act is projected into the pattern itself (78), which is why decoration seems to move. This creates an experience of “pleasurable frustration” (81) in trying to understand the animated decoration, allowing the object to succeed at being noticed and prolonging the experience. What it is like to encounter anything decorated around us is an ongoing, outward-directed experience that the object keeps alive.

Scarry’s use of pain runs in the opposite direction. The phenomenal experience she examines is aversiveness, which cannot be felt without the wish to be unfelt (290). An object’s meaning is to end the experience rather than extend it. Her example of the chair takes the shape of “perceived-pain-wished-gone” (290), and it succeeds when it relieves the problem of weight so routinely that nobody notices it (295). Only when its legs break does its failure become visible (296). Projection of our desires onto objects shapes a world that we integrate into our conscious experience.

Gell and Scarry place the experience in different locations, and this changes the direction in which each one’s cycle of object reciprocity begins. For Gell, the conscious experience found from captivation arises outside the body, in the animation of the pattern itself. The viewer, like a demon, is “hooked” by the pattern and held inside it (81). The experience is therefore located in the object first, and then moved back in, becoming a lasting part of the sense of self. Scarry on the other hand claims that felt-experience of sentience is interior, and its clearest form is found through pain (290). An object works as a carrier of this interior experience into the material world. Scarry describes this as a reversal of inside and outside, in which the external world gains part of the body’s sentience and the body gains some of the object’s relief (285).

Yet while each author’s starting points differ, they arrive at the same conclusion. Each author describes a cycle of reciprocation between inner experience and outer object. However, if the same cycle can be entered from the object or from the body then neither location can claim to be the sole home of phenomenal experience. The phenomenal experience is a relationship that runs through both.

Gell and Scarry both use phenomenality in order to argue that part of the integration of our conscious experience happens in the encounter between person and object, where the boundary between inner and outer is only relative. Yet, how can phenomenality allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? If the encounter between person and object is where part of the experience is integrated, then phenomenality may influence physical causality through the material world rather than through neuron activity. Scarry’s artifact is a “lever” that carries the force of creation back to the human site and remakes sentience there (307), and Gell’s pattern holds a viewer and shapes a biography (84). In both cases, felt experience becomes a physical thing, such as a chair or complex pattern, that changes what our bodies do. Still, neither author shows that felt experience can act without a physical medium. Phenomenality may therefore intervene only through objects, which leaves open whether it causes anything on its own.

Build Five: Comparing Reflexivity between Gell and Scarry

Both Gell and Scarry discuss the relationship between individuals and objects, in which individuals can develop awareness of themselves being aware, or reflexivity. In Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, Gell argues that patterns are a form of objects that are able to influence the people who encounter them. As a person interacts with the pattern, if they pause to question or make sense of the challenge at hand, this is a moment of reflexivity. Similarly, in The Body in Pain: The Making and Unmaking of the World, Scarry discusses how individuals reflect their thinking on the objects they create, a process called “projection.” The objects “act back” in a manner that reshapes the person’s thinking. Both Gell and Scarry point out the importance of inanimate objects in developing an individual’s relationship with the world, meaning that consciousness is not just an internalized phenomenon.

However, the authors diverge in how they analyze how exactly the objects function in accordance with an individual. Gell emphasizes that patterns play different types of roles. For some, analyzing a pattern can be considered as “performances, like dances, in which men could reveal their capability” (Gell 94). Questioning what forms a pattern or why it is the way it is depends on the prior knowledge of a person or simply their outlook, meaning different people can interpret patterns differently. For others, the purpose of a pattern is to be “apotropaic,” to keep away malevolent spirits by confusing them since they are unable to interpret the pattern as the creators might (Gell 83). As the individuals reflect on the patterns and realize that they are inquiring, they can become aware of the process, leading to reflexivity. Rather than responding to something familiar or following something as if going through the motions, reflexivity requires active thought over why something is different.

In contrast, Scarry focuses on how individuals can project their feelings and pain onto objects. She argues that “Aliveness, or awareness of aliveness, it will be argued, is in some very qualified sense projected out onto the object world” (Scarry 286). Furthermore, she states that artifacts are “the objectification of that awareness” (Scarry 289). An individual’s awareness is said to be expressed through an object, rather than an object influencing a person to express awareness, as Gell mentioned. Scarry uses the example of a woman making a coat to show that her creation is a way to project her human sentience (Scarry 307). Her desire to keep someone warm extends through the coat, which acts as her object/extension and transfers her desire to the person who wears the coat. Though she could keep a person warm herself, the coat accomplishes that same action indirectly despite the fact that it is an inanimate object and cannot feel the cold like the recipient of the coat can. However, the coat represents the maker’s awareness of the feeling of being cold, which is how Scarry implicitly takes on a different perspective regarding reflexivity.

Reflexivity is a quality of consciousness that both Gell and Scarry engage in their works, and both authors connect reflexivity to the agency of objects. From Gell’s perspective, objects challenge a person’s mind and encourage them to reflect harder on what they know (culturally or otherwise) and see. On the other hand, from Scarry’s perspective, individuals give a form to their awareness by transforming their inner thoughts into physical objects. Reflexivity requires more than just mere thinking; it requires questioning the surrounding world and how it fits in with what an individual knows. Scarry sees objects as extensions of oneself, whereas Gell sees objects as a way of challenging oneself. In a manner, both Scarry and Gell bring to light the idea that objects can continue to be influential over time, although it is more evident in Scarry’s writing compared to that of Gell. A painting can stir feelings in a new viewer years after its creation, and a pattern can still pose a challenge to one despite one’s return to it. Therefore, it can be argued that Gell and Scarry both frame the agency of objects as being long-lasting or perpetual, despite the differences in their description of the objects’ main functions.

Emily

In my earlier reading of Gell, I explored how complex patterns interrupt ordinary perception and make viewers aware of their own mental processes. I argued that reflexivity emerges when something that normally happens automatically becomes difficult, forcing us to notice how our consciousness operates. After reading Milton's Paradise Lost, however, I began to question whether awareness of our thoughts necessarily means understanding how they are shaped. Although Gell and Milton approach consciousness through different experiences, both explore the limitations of self-awareness. Gell shows how complex patterns expose the limits of perception, while Milton's portrayal of Eve raises questions about how accurately we understand our own thinking. Both suggest that consciousness is not always fully transparent to itself, but they differ in whether individuals recognize those limitations.

Gell explores reflexivity through the interruption of ordinary perception. Discussing the kolam, he explains that although he "'knows' (intellectually)" that a design consists of "four separate, but identical loops differently orientated," he finds it "extremely frustrating to attempt to abstract individual loops from the overall design" (85). What interests me is the difference between knowing something intellectually and actually being able to perceive it. Gell understands how the pattern is constructed, yet he cannot force himself to see its individual components simultaneously. This conflict interrupts what would otherwise be an automatic process of perception. Instead of simply observing the pattern, he becomes aware of his own struggle to understand it. His attention shifts from the object toward the limitations of his consciousness. Yet recognizing those limitations does not allow him to overcome them. Even though he understands the pattern's construction, he cannot completely control how he sees it. Gell therefore suggests that reflexivity can reveal the boundaries of our mental processes without giving us the ability to change them.

Milton, on the other hand, explores the limitations of reflexivity through Eve's encounter with the serpent in Paradise Lost. Unlike Gell, whose struggle makes him recognize the limits of his perception, Eve does not fully recognize how her thinking is being influenced. As the serpent challenges her beliefs about the forbidden fruit, Eve begins to question what she previously understood about knowledge and its consequences. What interests me is that Eve appears to trust her own understanding without recognizing how much the serpent's arguments have shaped it. Although she is conscious of her thoughts, she does not necessarily recognize the influences behind them. This contrasts with Gell, who becomes aware of the gap between what he knows and what he can perceive. While Gell's struggle exposes his limitations, Eve's confidence in her reasoning makes hers harder to recognize. Milton therefore suggests that being aware of our thoughts does not guarantee that we understand where they come from. Even when we examine our own thinking, we may overlook the assumptions and outside influences shaping it.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight: reflexivity does not necessarily provide complete access to our own consciousness. Gell reveals this through the gap between intellectual knowledge and perception, while Milton raises the possibility that our thoughts can be influenced in ways we fail to recognize. Initially, I understood reflexivity mainly as awareness emerging when an automatic mental process breaks down. Now, I see that recognizing our own thinking is not the same as understanding it accurately. Gell shows that self-awareness may reveal limitations we cannot overcome, while Milton suggests that some limitations may remain hidden even when we believe we are examining ourselves. Together, they lead to the idea that reflexivity is not a complete self-knowledge, but an imperfect attempt to examine the mind that is doing the examining.

Gell and Milton both use reflexivity to suggest that recognizing the gap between our intentions and experiences can influence how we respond, even without complete control over our consciousness. Yet, how can reflexivity allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? In Gell, recognizing a perceptual limitation creates an opportunity to redirect attention rather than continue responding automatically. Milton complicates this through Eve, whose failure to recognize the serpent's influence limits her ability to question her own thinking. They suggest that reflexivity may allow consciousness to influence subsequent actions by recognizing an initial response, considering alternatives, and choosing how to proceed. Although this does not prove that consciousness operates independently of physical causation, it suggests that self-awareness can influence the course of our actions.
