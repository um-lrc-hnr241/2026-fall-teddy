Rebecca

The role of non-living things in our world can be seen from many different angles. Many view them as less important to the living, while some look deeper into the true purpose of them. In Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, Alfred Gell challenges the assumption that agency belongs solely to conscious human beings. Gell defines agency as the ability to make something happen or produce an effect. What Gell presents in his text is not the idea of objects being conscious, but the idea that agency emerges within said objects, through relationships with living things. Without being conscious themselves, objects have the ability to influence human beings through perception, behavior, and emotions. This in turn brings up the question of the basic role of consciousness, if non living things play such a large part in these relationships.

Most of the time, children don’t enjoy the process of going to sleep. However, certain objects associated with this action assist in making it more appealing. Gell uses this example to illustrate how objects can directly influence human beings. He describes a bed with “a pillowcase richly embellished with spaceships, dinosaurs, or even polka dots, be they sufficiently jolly and attractive” (74). While at first glance these illustrations may seem decorative, they play a much larger role. They make bedtime more appealing to children, associating sleep with positive feelings stemming from the decorations. Its basic function is to provide a place to sleep, meaning that decorations are not necessary for it to fulfill its practical purpose. However, they change how the child views and interacts with the bed, giving the object an influence that goes beyond its original function. No one believes the bed has consciousness, yet it affects those who possess it. Agency stems not from the object itself, but from the relationship between said object and the person. The bed does not independently decide to influence the child. Instead, its decorations gain meaning through the child's own perception. An object gains agency through its interaction with living things. The bed is able to influence the child's conscious experience, showing that consciousness is not the only source of agency.

Later, Gell goes on to analyze the effect of a bamboo lime container. He says “any bamboo tube, or container of suitable size and shape, could do duty as a lime container…a plain container would hardly be as functional” (74). Gell claims that the decorated container tells much more about the “owner's” identity, serving as a sort of reflection. A relationship is created between the lime container and its owner, as the lime container becomes a representation of its owner's identity. The container's agency does not come from having a mind of its own, but from its ability to affect a social interaction, and shape another person's perception. The object and the people interacting with it work together to create meaning, making it difficult to determine how much influence comes from human consciousness versus the object itself.

The decorations on both these objects give additional meaning, and affect those who interact with them. Gell reveals that while he treats objects as non-conscious, those who encounter them are. The relationships built between objects and humans result in a sense of individual agency. Humans interact with these objects, and in turn, give meaning to them. They affect behavior, perception, and decision making. This conclusion raises the question of the role of consciousness in agency. If non living things are able to play a large role in the lives of those who hold a consciousness, what is the true role of consciousness? Gell does not directly answer this question, but his examples complicate the mindset that consciousness is a necessary part of every meaningful interaction. More directly, he points to objects as being heavily influential in these meaningful interactions. At the same time, it is possible to question whether objects possess agency, or whether humans simply give them meaning through their interactions with them. Gell's examples leave this distinction open, showing that non-conscious objects can still become part of human actions and social interactions. However, this conclusion does not answer the question on the true role of consciousness in the physical world, and its ability to create a new causal chain of its own.

If our attention and behavior can be directed by things outside our conscious control, how much agency does consciousness really have? We often think of consciousness as the part of ourselves responsible for controlling what we pay attention to, what we think about, and what we do. If a person consciously thinks about something, it seems natural to assume they would direct their own thoughts and behavior. Alfred Gell’s discussion of decorative objects and patterns in Art and Agency: Anthropological Theory, however, complicates this relationship.

Gell attributes a surprising amount of power to an object over the person observing it. He describes patterns as objects that attract people, hold their attention, influence their decisions, and therefore create relationships that continue over time. Through Gell’s descriptions of the ways decorative objects have the capacity to attract and influence people, he suggests that consciousness is not simply an independent force that directs our interactions with the world. Instead, consciousness emerges through an ongoing relationship between our minds and the things in the world around us.

Gell first establishes this relationship by arguing decoration can actively influence human behavior. He writes, “decorative patterns applied to artifacts attach people to things, and to the social projects those things entail” (Gell 74). His example, however, is surprisingly simple. A child might be more willing to go to bed if the bed has attractive sheets decorated with spaceships, dinosaurs, or even polka dots. The decoration does not physically force the child into bed, and the child doesn't consciously reason because these sheets have patterns I like, so I have decided to go to sleep. Nevertheless, the decoration may shape the child’s behavior if he associates the pattern with good sleep and rest. This example complicates the idea that conscious decisions are always the direct source of our actions. The child going to sleep may experience the bed as something they are choosing, but Gell points towards other forces that shape that decision.

Throughout his writing, Gell argues that consciousness does not necessarily make the observer perform an obvious movement. Instead, it notices an object or design, interprets it, remembers, and then returns with an action. In a way, Gell suggests that consciousness may function less like a commander and more like a processor of environmental cues that it can never perfectly master. What our conscious minds do depends on what the world outside gives it to process.

An object does not merely sit passively, it participates in producing and shaping a person’s response. A person’s consciousness may still participate, but its contribution is a response to influences that it did not create. Gell develops his ideas further through his concept of complex patterns as “unfinished business”. He argues that because complicated patterns resist complete understanding through visual inspection, they remain cognitively incomplete. According to Gell, “the process can continue interminably; the pattern is inexhaustible” (Gell 80). It is almost as if the observer cannot fully control their own attention. A person cannot just glance at the object, completely understand it, and move on. We are drawn to an object's complexities, and in turn it dictates and sustains our thoughts and actions. The conscious mind attempts to understand the pattern, but its complexity prevents the observer from easily reaching a final understanding. Even though the observer is consciously thinking about the pattern, the decision to continue examining it is shaped by the pattern itself. Conscious thought is directed by something outside of consciousness.

A person may consciously examine a pattern, but the pattern itself determines which parts are there for examination and why the mind continues returning to it. Gell’s writing offers an unusual perspective over whether consciousness is the main influence over our actions. He does not argue that consciousness is powerless; the observer is clearly thinking, perceiving, and attempting to understand. After all, the observer still interprets and responds to the object rather than simply obeying it, suggesting that outside influence does not completely eliminate conscious choice. Gell seems to locate a person's agency somewhere between the object and the person, rather than entirely in the conscious mind. Not only is the consciousness active, but it is also being acted upon. In Gell’s interpretation, consciousness partially participates in perception and action, but it does so in constant interaction with the world that acts upon it.

Build One

Professionals in scientific fields strive to uncover how the world around them works, such as how people function. For example, psychology explains that when an event takes place, neurons send signals to each other in the nervous system and release neurotransmitters that invoke a bodily response. Eventually, that response can lead to thoughts and emotions or moods. If there is a reason behind one’s mood, does that mean that a person has no complete control over it? If everything can be controlled by small processes occurring in the body, is there really consciousness?

Often, consciousness is assumed to be its own part of the brain that makes decisions such as reading this very paragraph. Instead, consciousness is more complex; it moves slower than the brain, so it cannot understand what is occurring because the brain has fast and slow processing. In Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, Alfred Gell discusses how people feel connected to patterns and further investigates how art can influence people. Gell’s understanding of consciousness raises an interesting question: if science can explain the physical processes behind a person taking an action, what makes an action intentional? Why is an action something a person does and not what happens to the person’s body? Considering this, Gell uses the example of a person intentionally raising their arm compared with the same physical movement, but this time involuntarily (124). Both situations involve an arm moving upward, with the same parts of the body working to make it happen (muscles and neurons). However, the two are not the same. The voluntary action occurred because the person wanted to, whereas the involuntary action just happened without the person wanting to. Unfortunately, the line distinguishing between voluntary and involuntary action is tricky. First, how can a bystander spot the difference from afar? Second, how does the person know their own intention?

Social context is important to humans, as a human’s immediate surroundings can have an impact on them, such as demonstrating how they must act. Gell demonstrates that patterns attach people to them because a “child may more readily be induced to go to bed… if the bed… [is] embellished with spaceships, dinosaurs, or even polka dots, be they sufficiently jolly and attractive” (74). Thus, patterns can be unconsciously associated with certain items or feelings. The same can go for human actions and intentions, done so by Gell’s description of Malakulan patterns. He explains that a pattern can represent a difficult journey filled with obstacles that must be overcome. Completing the pattern, or successfully making it through the maze, becomes a challenge in which a person demonstrates their ability (94). Malakulans did not view these patterns as “independent visual objects at all, but as performances, like dances, in which men could reveal their capability” (94). Meaning is not only derived from the object or pattern itself, but through the path taken by a person to create or solve it, and then their connection with it.

The social context regarding the Malakulans and their take on patterns allows for possible differentiation between voluntary and involuntary actions. If the men could reveal capability by completing patterns, there had to be some variation in mental activity between them while they felt connected to the pattern. This is due to the idea that if those acts were done by the body, then one person’s capability would be extremely similar to that of another person. The Malakulans would not have been able to perform because everyone would have had the same notions and processes.

Although the physical pattern provides the structure, the person gives the performance meaning through intentional action. Gell remarks that “a person and a person’s mind are not confined to particular spatio-temporal coordinates, but consist of a spread of biographical events and memories of events” (222). Therefore, a person and a pattern or object cannot be completely separated. Here is where consciousness exists, and it is how the Malakulans and people today are able to interpret the same exact pattern differently. Two individuals may be doing the same physical act, like raising an arm, but looking at the social context helps to determine whether the action was done consciously or not, and what makes the two different. While physical processes explain how an action took place, social context pairs with consciousness to give that action meaning, connecting a person’s intentions to objects and patterns nearby.

When ordinary humans and philosophers alike think about consciousness, they ask themselves - where is it derived from? What causes our actions? Does our mind have the final say? Alfred Gell approaches consciousness anthropologically rather than neuroscientifically, treating it not as a complex product of the decisions we make each second, but as something simpler. For him, consciousness emerges from the greater whole of events across our lives.

Gell relocates personhood outward, arguing that a person is "the sum of their relations… distributed over biographical time and space" (222). Personhood is not just simply confined to, “spatio-temporal coordinates,” (222) but rather consists of the "dispersed category of material objects, traces, and leavings"(222) that builds up over one's lifetime. The idea of personhood as the physical body and mind is understood as also including any other artifact in which agency can be inferred. The world does not need "consciousness" to be present for one's agency to be felt. A heroic act or piece of art left behind in someone's reputation continues to affect the ongoing world. What we think of as present, conscious actions are not what solely builds our outward character. Whereas Gell says “biological death” (222) may occur, a person's mind and its agency remains. While the Libet experiment may be focused on the biological process of decision making that shapes consciousness, Gell is more focused on the social aspects. Gell isn't interested in whether the mind lags behind the brain; he's asking whether the mind was ever the right place to look at all.

For Gell, personhood is distributed across our relationships and actions rather than contained within a single bounded self. Consciousness follows the same logic. If personhood extends outward into what we build and leave behind, then consciousness can't be confined to a private, moment-by-moment stream of decisions either. The extended life one builds around oneself is where consciousness really takes life. He asks why our own decision-making and freedom matter, when we are ultimately remembered by the products we create rather than by our internal experience. This matters enormously for the "does consciousness really matter" debate that Gell often circles.

Gell grounds this reframing in his response to an idea from Dennett about the 'homunculi' - if we search for a smaller person inside us who makes up consciousness, we will endlessly wonder who controls that little person and on. If there is no place to find out where and how we really control our own ideas and actions, then where is the idea of consciousness coming from? Gell offers to redefine consciousness as a story, not a little human. The mind is not the headquarters of consciousness but rather one region of a larger system of body, social relations, history, and objects. This idea is clever, but it's worth asking whether Gell has actually solved the endless loop problem or just relocated it. If consciousness is found in this system, we can still ask who or what controls the system. Gell just widens the frame until the original question feels less urgent. He also turns a common idea that our inner selves project our outward appearance on its head, saying, “our inner personhood seems to consist of replications of what we are externally.” (222) Consciousness is not the origin of all behavior, but rather just a reflection of it. If our personhood is determined externally, then this diverts our understanding of consciousness to the exterior.

This system answers the “does consciousness really matter” question not by tackling it head on but by reframing the idea. Instead of a definite yes or no, Gell's ideas tell us that there is little point in finding a singular point of "consciousness" because our consciousness is spread out across everything that makes up our lives. He repeatedly uses the term “agency” and how things “testify to agency and patienthood during a biographical career” (222) as his way of defining anything that fits within the system. It is our “agency” over objects and actions that makes up personhood, not our consciousness over their meaning.

Gell argues that the system of our lives cannot be understood by capturing one single point, but by taking a journey across the entirety of it. Yet he still doesn't answer questions about if consciousness really matters. Gell describes a picture of how one may see personhood but he never delves deeper into if our conscious mind is really the one holding the brush.

Build 1

It is often imagined that consciousness is a voice trapped inside the walls of our brain, but what if it extends beyond the body? Alfred Gell’s discussion in Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory reframes this question by challenging the distinction between the “inner” and “outer” self as only relative. He argues that a person is not confined to a particular body but can extend through relationships, memories, objects, and material traces (Gell 104). This argument becomes significant when considering whether consciousness is solely responsible for human agency, or whether agency is spread across a much larger network.

Gell begins by discussing Daniel Dennett’s criticism of the homunculus, the supposed “little person” inside the brain who perceives the world and makes decisions. This metaphor creates a problem: if an observer sits inside the brain, who observes the observer? And who controls the controller? (Hoffman). Gell’s discussion opposes the idea of consciousness as a hidden person sitting inside us. Rather than searching for the “real” person inside the brain, he encourages us to consider the person as relationships with the world. The “person” is less like a single point and more like a web, extending beyond the physical body—a challenge to Gell’s concept of distributed personhood or the assumption of a single internal source responsible for perception, decision-making and thoughts. If there is no “little person” making decisions, then consciousness can not be simply understood as an internal observer directing behavior.

Instead, Gell writes that a person and their mind are not confined to particular “spatio-temporal coordinates,” but consist of a “spread” of biographical events, memories, and objects (Gell 104). The word “spread” is significant because it suggests that personhood has no fixed location. A person creates their traces through their possessions, memories, relationships, and creativity, which continue to exist even after the individual is no longer physically present. However, this raises a question of whether consciousness has causal power. If physical processes in the brain can explain behavior, then consciousness might appear almost like a passenger, experiencing what happens without influencing it—though a passenger still experiences and interprets the journey, so the metaphor does not make consciousness meaningless. Gell’s argument does not solve this problem, but complicates how it is framed. If the person is distributed beyond the brain, then perhaps agency cannot be understood simply by looking for a single internal source.

Art makes this especially visible. A painting can carry traces of an artist’s intentions into the future, remaining long after its creator has died, continuing to influence viewers. The artist does not need to remain present for their agency to remain active through the artwork. The artwork becomes part of a larger web of agency, carrying something of the person into places and times where they cannot go (Gell 221-223). This is not unique to art; Gell does not argue that objects are conscious in the same way humans are. Instead, objects can carry an extension of human agency through memories, and emotional reactions (Gell 76). A portrait, for instance, might preserve the presence of someone who is no longer alive; a personal object might evoke memories and behavior. In each scenario, something outside the body participates in the continuing mosaic of a person—agency may belong more broadly to the network of people and objects surrounding it, while consciousness remains one part of that process.

Still, Gell’s argument leaves open where this agency actually belongs. If agency belongs to the artist, how do their intentions remain after the artist is gone? If agency belongs to the object itself, this is difficult to explain as an object does not have intentions or consciousness of its own. Perhaps, then, if agency emerges through the artist, the work, and the viewer together, it does not need one specific location. Instead, agency may emerge through these connections. This fits Gell’s theory that a person is composed of different actions, responses and intentions, making agency distributed rather than located in one place.

Gell changes the way we view consciousness but does not resolve the debate. Instead, his idea challenges the boundary the debate depends on. His image of a web of traces replaces the idea of consciousness being a sealed room and art makes this vivid: intentions leave the mind and continue affecting the world through objects decades later. Whether this means consciousness itself is causally powerful remains unresolved. Gell suggests that understanding human agency may require us to explore beyond the brain and toward the network of people, objects, memories, and relationships through which a person continues to exist and act upon.

A trap usually works because there is something preventing its victim from escaping. It could be a cage, a barrier, or something else that physically holds the victim in place. The trap Gell describes, however, does not actually restrain its victim at all. In his example of Celtic knotwork, an evil spirit becomes “so fascinated by the entwined braids as to suffer from a paralysis of the will” (84). The spirit originally has its own “malevolent plan,” but after encountering the pattern, it loses interest in that plan and becomes “stuck in the coils.” What is strange is that these coils exist only as part of the pattern. Nothing physically prevents the demon from leaving, yet somehow it cannot pull its attention away. Gell’s example suggests that being trapped can involve more than losing physical freedom; it can also mean losing control over what the mind chooses to focus on. But if nothing is physically stopping the demon, what is actually preventing it from continuing with its original plan?

This becomes especially interesting because the demon does not enter the situation without a will of its own. Gell tells us that it already has a “malevolent plan,” meaning that the demon intends to do something before encountering the knotwork. Yet fascination with the pattern is powerful enough to interrupt this intention completely. The demon has not necessarily forgotten what it was doing because Gell instead says it has “los[es] interest” in it. This creates a strange separation between intending to do something and actually having control over whether that intention becomes an action. If the demon is conscious of what it originally intends to do but can still be pulled away from it, then consciousness does not necessarily give it control over its behavior. This makes me wonder what actually causes the demon’s attention to shift in the first place.

Gell calls the result a “paralysis of the will,” which seems almost contradictory because the demon is still mentally active. It notices the knotwork and becomes fascinated enough to follow its intertwined pattern. Rather than shutting down the demon’s mind, this “paralysis” seems to result from an excess of attention: the demon becomes so mentally engaged with the pattern that its awareness interferes with its ability to carry out its original intention. In this case, being more conscious of something does not necessarily give the demon greater control. Its attention instead becomes the very thing that limits its ability to act. Instead of helping the demon carry out its intention, consciousness seems to become part of what prevents that intention from being an action.

Gell’s description of the demon becoming “stuck in the coils” further blurs the distinction between mental and physical restraint. The demon’s fascination functions like a physical restraint even though nothing actually prevents it from leaving. The pattern does not need to force the demon to stop because the demon’s own response to it does the work. This complicates the question of where the power of the trap actually comes from. The pattern cannot literally decide to distract the demon, but the demon also does not seem completely free to decide what deserves its attention. The trap therefore depends on both the pattern and the demon’s response to it. Without the demon’s fascination, the pattern could not trap it, but the demon does not appear to choose that fascination either.

Gell seems to assume that consciousness can influence behavior without necessarily being fully in control of it. The demon’s awareness of the pattern changes what it ultimately does, yet the demon does not appear to choose what captures its attention in the first place. At first, I saw the demon’s fascination as the cause of its changed behavior, but this does not explain what causes that fascination in the first place. The demon’s fascination produces a physical consequence because it no longer carries out its “malevolent plan,” but that fascination was itself triggered by encountering the pattern. This makes it difficult to tell how much of the demon’s change actually comes from its consciousness and how much has already been set in motion by the pattern. If consciousness can change what the demon does while also being shaped by something outside of its control, where does the demon’s own conscience actually begin to make a difference?
