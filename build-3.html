Rebecca

When I first encountered Gell’s Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, I understood the relationships between objects and living things as relatively uncomplicated: objects acquire agency through their relationships with people, and in turn affect the outside world. When studying the reading more, this analysis seems to be incomplete. Gell's argument is not only that objects have their own autonomy, but that their influence is continuous, stretching across past, present, and future. An object's effect does not end when the original action ends; it persists, and can change in meaning as the people around it change. This realization bestows greater power on the material world. Fundamentally, Gell's work can be seen through the lens of reconsolidation. Just as a memory is altered each time it is recalled, identity according to Gell is never fixed, and its revision does not take place entirely in a person's mind. Through reconsolidation, the past is carried into the present, where it can be reshaped and go on to affect the future. Identities develop through evolving relationships with the material world, as objects change in meaning alongside the people who interact with them.

Gell builds this point through the idea of unfinished business. He describes the relationship between a carpet and owner as “inexhaustible” (80): this connection is never fully resolved but continuous throughout the owner's life. The key to this continuity is that the exchange between the living and the material world is never equal. There always seems to be a sort of “delay” or “lag” (81) between said transactions, meaning the interaction is never truly closed. This lag links Gell's argument to reconsolidation. Over time, the object becomes part of how the owner understands themselves and how others understand them, creating a feedback loop in which it becomes difficult to separate the person's developing identity from the object. Identity, in this sense, is not stored privately and then expressed outward; it is repeatedly revised through contact with things.

Gell's concept of the distributed person extends this point from a single object to a person’s whole surrounding. He highlights the difficulty of distinguishing between what “people do as persons, and what people do as things” (124). Where most of us would easily separate a person from an object, Gell suggests that, on a closer examination, it becomes hard to tell whether an action stems from a person's own consciousness or from some outside influence. The distributed person argues that a person's existence is not confined strictly to their body, but instead made up of various networks from their surroundings including relationships, objects, and circumstances. One might object that the carpet itself never changes and only the owner’s mind does. But Gell’s point is that the two can not be cleanly separated. Therefore, a person's identity will never be stable; it is constantly being modified and transformed as a result of the outside world.

Gell's discussion of apotropaic art reveals the mechanism behind this continuity. Apotropaic objects are designed to ward off evil, and Gell describes them as protecting an agent from a hostile recipient. Many do so by captivating this evil and expelling it. A malevolent spirit becomes absorbed in trying to escape the difficult pattern and work out the design, making it unable to pass. The pattern protects by creating unfinished business for the very thing it guards against. The same lag that keeps the owner bound to the carpet becomes a trap, since the demon, like the owner, can never fully exhaust the pattern. Although Gell writes about spirits, I would suggest that this mechanism may persist today in a different form. Specific stimuli exist as a way to ward off which can be defined as “inner demons.” This could be a decorated surface, a story line, or even technology. In both modern day and past interpretations, patterns protect by creating unfinished business for the very thing it guards against. The actual action of the artist creating the art is finished, yet the object’s protective work is not; it continues long after its creation, every time a threat is present. This threat could be seen as an outer demon, or as mentioned, an inner demon inside our mind. The protective object carries the past with it, created due to a prior need to ward against evil. This past continues to shape present and future encounters. The object functions almost like an externalized memory: it preserves an early concern and reactivates it in new circumstances. The past is not simply remembered, but continually put back into use, shaping the identity of those it protects.

The labyrinth shows what this entanglement looks like from the inside, when the mind caught in the pattern is a human one. The Cretan labyrinth evokes a navigational problem, without actually offering one: it creates a cognitive obstacle even though there is a way through. Yet where the demon remains trapped, the human viewer can work through the pattern by grasping the “iterative rule in connecting up lines and points” (88). What matters here is not only that the viewer is eventually able to untangle the maze, but that their understanding is rebuilt in stages through repeated engagement. The confusion draws them in, and the search leads them to uncover the structure beneath. Each return to the pattern revises what the viewer previously believed about it, much as each recall of a memory revises the memory, showing how consciousness continually reorganizes its understanding through encounters with the material world.

Taken together, these examples push Gell’s account of objects beyond the idea of a single interaction. Through the lens of reconsolidation, the relationship between people and objects becomes a continuous process in which the past shapes what comes next, and objects take on a greater role than I first recognized. This in turn raises the question of the power of living things themselves. Unfinished business says that something from the past has the ability to continue to matter in the present, even when the original agent is no longer acting. Actions may be finished, but effects are not. Gell's example asks how consciousness responds to effects that originate outside itself, and whether the revision of the self is something we carry alone, or something we undertake together with the objects around us.

Build 3

Narration: coordination of successive states, often over extended stretches of time, into a sequence with a design that gives purpose.

If agency belongs to the artist, how do their intentions remain after the artist is gone? If consciousness is understood as immediate thought within an individual mind, then an artist’s intentions should end when the artist dies. However, Alfred Gell’s Art and Agency complicates this assumption by showing how agency can continue through objects and relationships. Artwork can affect others long after its creation. Narration, defined as the “coordination of successive states, often over extended stretches of time, into a sequence with a design that gives purpose,” connects an artist’s intention to its later effects. I will argue that if the sequence an intention sets in motion counts as part of consciousness, then the artist's death ends an interior act but not the consciousness that began it.

Gell helps explain why artwork can matter after its creator is gone. He describes artwork as indexes because they provide material traces through which an agent can be recognized and their agency inferred. He also distinguishes consciousness from its effects, writing that “aesthetic judgements are only interior mental acts; art objects, on the other hand, are produced and circulated in the external physical and social world” (Gell 4). Although consciousness remains interior, its material traces continue to affect others. Gell does not claim this extends consciousness, but his distinction raises the question of whether its effects can outlast the individual. This raises a problem: if the artwork continues to produce effects without the artist, in what sense are those effects still a part of the artist's consciousness?

Narration makes this connection across time significant. An artist forms an intention, acts on it by creating artwork, and the artwork enters the world where other people encounter and respond to it. Each state is shaped by what came before. The idea of a "distributed person" clarifies this continuation: a person's intentions can become embedded in objects, memories, heirlooms, legacies, and other traces. Artwork can therefore become part of the artist's distributed presence, carrying their intentions into encounters that occur later. Narration connects these encounters as successive states across time, showing how an artist's agency can move through different people, objects, and contexts rather than ending with the artist's physical absence.

We can focus on the artist as the source of an action, but we can also focus on the object through which that agency continues to have effects. The artwork does not need to possess consciousness for this to occur. Instead, agency becomes distributed across the artist, an object, and those who encounter it. Gell argues that “art objects are not ‘self-sufficient’ agents, but only ‘secondary’ agents in conjunction with certain specific (human) associates” (Gell 17). An artwork's effects depend on human relationships. Those who preserve, interpret, or display it participate in determining what it does. This connects to the distributed person: the artist's agency extends through the objects they create and the relationships those objects produce. Gell’s discussion of decoration makes this process concrete. He writes that “decorative patterns applied to artefacts attach people to things, and to the social projects those things entail” (Gell 74). His example is a child who is more willing to go to bed because the sheets are decorated with spaceships. The child is not thinking of the designer at all, yet the designer's intention still shapes what the child does. That is hard to reconcile with consciousness as immediate thought. Artwork therefore does not merely preserve the past; it can generate what happens next. In terms of narration, the artist creates the object, another person encounters it, and that encounter can produce a new action.

This matters for consciousness because the consequences of an intentional thought can extend beyond the moment in which that thought occurs. Artwork can similarly carry an intention into relationships its creator will never experience. The trace may be more than evidence of what consciousness has done in the world; it may be part of consciousness itself. What survives is not necessarily the artist's mind, but a sequence initiated by their intentional action. Other people can give artwork new consequences, making the artist’s original intention part of a larger narrative. However, this raises an objection: if later recipients determine what the artwork means and does, can its effects still be attributed to the artist? Narration offers one way to understand this connection, since a sequence is defined by its “design that gives purpose,” not by one person’s continued awareness of it.

Ultimately, Gell suggests that an artist’s agency can outlast their physical presence, even if their consciousness does not. Through objects and the people who encounter them, an original intention can shape events beyond the artist’s control. Narration connects these effects across time, but whether this sequence becomes part of consciousness remains uncertain. I began by assuming that an artist’s thought ends with their death, but Gell complicates this view. Perhaps what survives is neither the artist’s consciousness nor their intention unchanged, but an ongoing sequence that takes on new meanings through the people who encounter it.

Build 3: Phenomenality

Phenomenality: integrated experiences (like that of color) create a sense of “what it is like to be me” that does not reduce to neural activity alone.

Initially, I understood Gell’s description of consciousness as consisting of the greater world of objects around us. While I think that is still true, it is much more complex than that. The world around us clearly shapes our sense of who we are. Gell, though, is after something more specific. We project our own agency onto objects, and those objects in turn help us feel the things we intrinsically associate with "consciousness." Gell specifically uses decorative patterns to get his point across. Gell’s descriptions of decorative patterns supply a concrete case of the phenomenality quality of consciousness. Gell uses patterns to show that "me" arguably isn’t necessarily located inside a person. Our own perception of a pattern's livelihood helps us understand what it means to be ourselves. Gell layers his argument by first talking about how patterns appear animated, then how complex patterns cannot be understood by just their composition, and finally by relating our experiences with those patterns back to self.

Gell begins on pages 78 and 79 by talking about symmetry. While for an epiphenomenalist, symmetry may just be about simple geometric shapes, Gell points out that patterns seem to move because the eye must travel from motif to motif and mentally layer each one onto the next, an activity that comes from the viewer's own perception. Although a cognitive explanation must account for this, Gell doesn't call the result just an illusion. He compares this to the sun moving across the sky. While the sun does not move across the sky, the observer does, the description of the sun “moving” is not an error. Humanity says the “sun comes up”, describing the sun as a moving object, because there is a real relationship between perceiver and object. The mechanism can be fully described and the felt animation still needs its own account. However this leaves the question of if the felt animation comes from a sequence of scanning movements, maybe our experience is just each of these movements added together? Like how a sequence of still frames make a larger moving video.

Gell’s Kolam example on pages 85-86 tests that exact hypothesis because the sum of these parts fails to appear. The design looks like one continuous line but is really four loops each rotated 90 degrees and layered next to each other. Gell notes though, that even after knowing this a human can maybe notice each loop separately: finding one may be possible, but not multiple. The knowledge of the parts does not change how the sum looks. Gell doesn’t make the comparison, but I found this exactly like the Mary the scientist example. While having color knowledge, she cannot truly understand until she has experience of seeing them. Mary has the knowledge without the experience; the kolam viewer has both, yet still cannot see the figure as the sum of its loops. Both support the same conclusion that our integrated experience has a unity that decomposition cannot reproduce.

A unity that survives the full knowledge of its parts is still not a finished event. This is what Gell means when he says “Unfinished Business” (81). The eye can always rest on a new relation, and only by experiencing things can we truly understand what they mean to the “me”. This makes the relationship between humans and objects “inexhaustible”. A patterned object becomes part of how its own is present to themselves over time. “What it's like to be me” is built up through repeated encounters with these objects and patterns. Gell defines this when he says we are confined to “spatio-temporal coordinates” (222), as we build our “me” around the objects which we have relationships with. But this makes it unclear whether this “me” can be found inside someone at all.

Gell’s exploration of this thought at the end of the text is where he begins to locate this Phenomenality away from the “me” and more outward. Searching inside a person for a “me” he says is like Dennett's homunculi (222), so the inner self is really a replication of everything “from which agency or personhood can be abducted” (222). This makes this feeling of “what it is like to be me” constituted not just of the conscious self but also by patterned things and the relationships we have with them across our life. So while yes, we feel "conscious" because experiences cannot be reduced to neuroactivity alone, it was never actually confined to it.

Build Three: Reflexivity

My initial interpretation of Gell’s Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory focused on how agency belonged specifically to the person acting on an object. However, the writing explains that agency can extend to an object despite it not necessarily having consciousness. According to Gell, agency lies in a network of social connections, and objects are a part of this network. Objects can invoke emotions in a person, distributing thoughts and intentions. As this process of agency occurs, the body can find itself in a strange position: acting with reflexivity. Reflexivity is defined as awareness that one is aware of something different or of one's environment. It occurs during moments of stress, crisis, or perceived threat when an intentional decision is of better use compared to a non-impulsive decision. Gell goes into depth about the role of patterns in a person’s life relating to the agency described previously, which can trigger reflexivity.

Patterns are considered objects that can be held dear to a person and can influence the person’s actions. Gell’s writing depicts a child feeling more inclined to sleep in a bed covered in a jolly pattern, such as dinosaurs (Gell 74), than one without such a pattern. Similarly, a person may prefer a cup with a specific set of shapes and colors compared to a plain one. Here, the pattern acts by causing a person to admire it and hold it more dearly to them, displaying agency through the object rather than only through the person whose agency allows them to observe the object. Despite influencing decisions, the responses do not necessarily require an understanding of the pattern’s significance, suggesting that the agency of an object on a person can precede their conscious reflection.

Aside from being aesthetically captivating, a pattern can represent a difficult journey filled with obstacles that must be overcome. This relates to a pattern’s intricacy and composition, for it can confuse the one who looks at it— a small moment of crisis. For example, a diagram of winding lines that overlap with one another repeatedly can be puzzling, for where does it begin and end? Completing the pattern becomes a challenge, as described for the Malakulans, in which the patterns were not seen as “independent visual objects at all, but as performances, like dances, in which men could reveal their capability” (Gell 94). Compared to children who may not understand why they are drawn to sleep, the Malakulans are aware that there is something intentional about the pattern displayed and, through their attempt at creating a solution, become aware of how they are examining it rather than just perceiving the object as a whole without looking past its appearance.

Gell further discusses the role of patterns in testing the capabilities. In Malakulan culture, on the passage to the afterlife, ghosts go along a road where a completed geometrical figure is incomplete. Seeing that, the ghost “loses his track and cannot find it. Only a knowledge of the completed geometrical figure can release him” (Gell 93). The ghost gains awareness as it learns a solution from the pattern on the ground and uses it to be released. The difference between the successful and failed ghosts lies in their ability to interpret. Recognition does not guarantee a solution. The agency of the pattern not only presents an obstacle, but also reveals the intention of the guardian ghost, which is to safeguard the afterlife. For the ghosts that may be aware there is a pattern but do not necessarily have the “knowledge” required to complete it, they are unable to act upon their reflexivity. In reality, it can lead to frustration and no remedy for the moment of crisis.

Gell’s articulation of reflexivity can be used to depict a quality of consciousness. Reflexivity does not only focus on the world around a person, but emphasizes how the person’s own knowledge and perspective shape their surroundings. The ghost problem illustrates how recognition and resolution are distinct from one another. It demonstrates that objects can contain information not easily visible, and this concept helps one to reconsider the boundaries of agency, which emerges from the connections between an object and people. Awareness, as a part of reflexivity, can help one recognize a problem, but it may not always provide a resolution, meaning the person may be aware but cannot act on it.

Emily

In my first reading of Gell, I focused on how complex patterns capture attention and create what he calls a “paralysis of the will.” I understood this mainly as viewers becoming trapped in the attempt to mentally reconstruct a pattern they cannot fully grasp. After rereading Gell, however, I became less interested in the fact that the viewer gets trapped and more interested in what happens to their consciousness during that experience. The viewer does not simply struggle to understand the pattern but may also become aware of that struggle as it is happening. This changed my understanding of Gell because his patterns seem capable of provoking reflexivity, or awareness that one is aware. The difficulty of perceiving a pattern does not necessarily produce reflexivity on its own, but it can make viewers notice mental processes they would otherwise overlook. More importantly, Gell suggests that becoming aware of those processes does not necessarily mean gaining control over them.

Gell begins to reveal this relationship when he describes “‘seeing’” simple geometric figures as “mentally intending or projecting the construction of the figure, line by line” (85). His use of “intending” makes seeing sound less passive than it normally feels. The viewer does not simply receive an image but mentally participates in constructing it. With simple figures, this activity happens so easily that there is little reason to notice it. Complex patterns, however, make this process more difficult. Gell's discussion of the kolam is an example of this. He “‘knows’ (intellectually)” that the design consists of “four separate, but identical loops differently orientated,” yet finds it “extremely frustrating to attempt to abstract individual loops from the overall design” (85). His use of “intellectually” creates an important distinction between knowledge and perception. He already knows how the pattern is constructed, so his frustration does not come from lacking information. Instead, he cannot make his perception follow what he knows. He can isolate one loop through effort, but when attempting to perceive multiple loops simultaneously, they disappear back into the overall design. This difference between knowing and seeing is what makes the kolam particularly interesting in relation to reflexivity. Gell becomes aware not only of the pattern but also of his own inability to perceive it in a particular way. The pattern makes him confront the process of seeing rather than simply the object being seen.

While Gell’s discussion of the kolam shows how patterns can interrupt perception, his later discussion of maze-like patterns extends this idea to intention and action. He describes a maze-like pattern as creating an “impasse” where, without “knowledge of the completed geometrical figure,” the dead person “will never find the road” and “never reaches the land of the dead” (93). What stood out to me here is that the pattern does more than capture someone’s attention. It actually prevents them from completing something they are trying to do. The dead intend to continue their journey, but the structure of the pattern interrupts that intention and keeps them trapped. I think this helps explain why Gell gives patterns a kind of agency. The pattern does not have to be alive or conscious to influence what someone is able to do. It can create an obstacle that forces someone to stop and struggle with something that would otherwise happen automatically. In that moment, the struggle itself can become noticeable, creating the possibility for reflexivity. Even if someone becomes aware of what is stopping them, that awareness does not necessarily mean they can overcome it.

Through the kolam and the maze, Gell shows how patterned objects can influence what people perceive and how they act. Although Gell does not explicitly discuss reflexivity, I think his examples reveal how awareness of our own consciousness can emerge when something interrupts our intentions. What makes this important to his argument is that patterns do not simply exist for us to observe or understand. They can actively shape our experiences by making us confront the limitations of our own perception and control. I now understand Gell's argument as suggesting that even our awareness of ourselves can be influenced by objects outside of us.
