Build 3: Phenomenality

Phenomenality: integrated experiences (like that of color) create a sense of “what it is like to be me” that does not reduce to neural activity alone.

Initially, I understood Gell’s description of consciousness as consisting of the greater world of objects around us. While I think that is still true, it is much more complex than that. The world around us clearly shapes our sense of who we are. Gell, though, is after something more specific. We project our own agency onto objects, and those objects in turn help us feel the things we intrinsically associate with "consciousness." Gell specifically uses decorative patterns to get his point across. Gell’s descriptions of decorative patterns supply a concrete case of the phenomenality quality of consciousness. Gell uses patterns to show that "me" arguably isn’t necessarily located inside a person. Our own perception of a pattern's livelihood helps us understand what it means to be ourselves. Gell layers his argument by first talking about how patterns appear animated, then how complex patterns cannot be understood by just their composition, and finally by relating our experiences with those patterns back to self.

Gell begins on pages 78 and 79 by talking about symmetry. While for an epiphenomenalist, symmetry may just be about simple geometric shapes, Gell points out that patterns seem to move because the eye must travel from motif to motif and mentally layer each one onto the next, an activity that comes from the viewer's own perception. Although a cognitive explanation must account for this, Gell doesn't call the result just an illusion. He compares this to the sun moving across the sky. While the sun does not move across the sky, the observer does, the description of the sun “moving” is not an error. Humanity says the “sun comes up”, describing the sun as a moving object, because there is a real relationship between perceiver and object. The mechanism can be fully described and the felt animation still needs its own account. However this leaves the question of if the felt animation comes from a sequence of scanning movements, maybe our experience is just each of these movements added together? Like how a sequence of still frames make a larger moving video.

Gell’s Kolam example on pages 85-86 tests that exact hypothesis because the sum of these parts fails to appear. The design looks like one continuous line but is really four loops each rotated 90 degrees and layered next to each other. Gell notes though, that even after knowing this a human can maybe notice each loop separately: finding one may be possible, but not multiple. The knowledge of the parts does not change how the sum looks. Gell doesn’t make the comparison, but I found this exactly like the Mary the scientist example. While having color knowledge, she cannot truly understand until she has experience of seeing them. Mary has the knowledge without the experience; the kolam viewer has both, yet still cannot see the figure as the sum of its loops. Both support the same conclusion that our integrated experience has a unity that decomposition cannot reproduce.

A unity that survives the full knowledge of its parts is still not a finished event. This is what Gell means when he says “Unfinished Business” (81). The eye can always rest on a new relation, and only by experiencing things can we truly understand what they mean to the “me”. This makes the relationship between humans and objects “inexhaustible”. A patterned object becomes part of how its own is present to themselves over time. “What it's like to be me” is built up through repeated encounters with these objects and patterns. Gell defines this when he says we are confined to “spatio-temporal coordinates” (222), as we build our “me” around the objects which we have relationships with. But this makes it unclear whether this “me” can be found inside someone at all.

Gell’s exploration of this thought at the end of the text is where he begins to locate this Phenomenality away from the “me” and more outward. Searching inside a person for a “me” he says is like Dennett's homunculi (222), so the inner self is really a replication of everything “from which agency or personhood can be abducted” (222). This makes this feeling of “what it is like to be me” constituted not just of the conscious self but also by patterned things and the relationships we have with them across our life. So while yes, we feel "conscious" because experiences cannot be reduced to neuroactivity alone, it was never actually confined to it.
