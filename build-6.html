Build 6

Build 6: Group build

In Alfred Gell’s Art and Agency, he explores how one's experience with objects and patterns reflect each of the five different qualities of consciousness.

Gell’s description of decorative objects explains how our phenomenal feeling of consciousness cannot be reduced to neural activity alone. As we are captivated by decorative patterns we more deeply understand ourselves. “What it's like to be me” is built up through repeated encounters with these objects and patterns. Gell locates that feeling outwards, as when we give objects agency, we find our phenomenal feeling in them as well as within ourselves. That integration happens in the encounter between person and object, where the boundary between inner and outer is only relative. So is “me” really found within ourselves?

If our sense of self is shaped by objects, over time, we develop personhood. Gell explains how patterns prompt individuals to delve deeper, causing them to question why they are captivated or confused by what is observed. Reflexivity causes one to use their prior knowledge and their changing perspective as they consider a challenge, actively thinking. Scarry approaches objects differently, focusing on how individuals give form to their awareness by transforming thoughts into objects. Ultimately, objects influence people over a long time, and Gell suggests that reflexivity does not require one to act on or understand a challenge.

Moreover, through Gell’s discussion of complex patterns, we initially understood reflexivity as becoming aware of our mental processes when ordinary perception is interrupted. However, we later recognized that experiencing difficulty does not automatically lead to self- awareness. Comparing Gell with Milton’s Paradise Lost complicated this idea further, revealing that even when we consciously examine our thoughts, we may fail to recognize the outside influences shaping them. Together, these readings suggest that reflexivity does not guarantee complete understanding or control over our consciousness. Instead, it may reveal how much of our own thinking remains beyond our awareness.

These limitations in the ability to understand one's own thinking raise questions about the control we have over the decisions we make. Through our examination of deliberation, we have come to understand that consciousness involves more than simply considering different possibilities. Gell demonstrates that deliberation allows individuals to reconsider their approaches when faced with limitations, rather than responding automatically. However, comparing Gell with Milton complicates this understanding by showing that the ability to imagine alternatives does not guarantee freedom in choosing between them. External influences, personal desires, and fears shape how individuals evaluate their opinions, even when they consciously deliberate. Deliberation creates the possibility of choice, but does not necessarily grant individuals complete control over their decisions.

If decisions are difficult to be made in the present, what if memory can influence them? We discovered that an object's past never fully closes, which can connect back to the quality of reconsolidation. Through the unfinished business of patterns such as the kolam tattoo and the carpet, the past is carried into the present, where it can be reshaped to affect the future. However, whether an encounter produces revision or paralysis depends on what the mind brings to it. The kolam tattoo had the ability to both lead a woman to her kin, and trap a demon who meets it as a stranger. Comparing Gell with Scarry, we then saw how objects carry the perceptions of those who made them, showing how reconsolidation can happen between minds, not just within them. This led us to believe that consciousness has the ability to shape the world long after the physical interaction has ended. So how can specific patterns be so influential?

We initially learned that objects can carry an artist's agency through the way people interact with and interpret them, even in ways the artist may not have intended. Then, by incorporating Scarry, we understood how imagination can turn human experiences and desires into physical objects that continue to serve a purpose beyond their original creation. As our ideas developed, we began to recognize the difference between an intention living on and consciousness itself continuing to exist. Ultimately, we came to understand that even though consciousness may come to an end, its influence doesn't necessarily disappear. Through narration, separate experiences and intentions can be coordinated over time into a meaningful sequence with purpose, allowing a person's influence to continue shaping people's experiences, relationships, and the meanings they create long after someone is gone.

Throughout our analysis of Gell, we kept coming back to one main question: How can our consciousness be developed through art and agency? In Builds 3, 4, and 5, we explored how an artist's intentions can live on through their artwork and continue to affect others long after it is created.
