Build 1

Build 1: Rebecca

The role of non-living things in our world can be seen from many different angles. Many view them as less important to the living, while some look deeper into the true purpose of them. In Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory, Alfred Gell challenges the assumption that agency belongs solely to conscious human beings. Gell defines agency as the ability to make something happen or produce an effect. What Gell presents in his text is not the idea of objects being conscious, but the idea that agency emerges within said objects, through relationships with living things. Without being conscious themselves, objects have the ability to influence human beings through perception, behavior, and emotions. This in turn brings up the question of the basic role of consciousness, if non living things play such a large part in these relationships.

Most of the time, children don’t enjoy the process of going to sleep. However, certain objects associated with this action assist in making it more appealing. Gell uses this example to illustrate how objects can directly influence human beings. He describes a bed with “a pillowcase richly embellished with spaceships, dinosaurs, or even polka dots, be they sufficiently jolly and attractive” (74). While at first glance these illustrations may seem decorative, they play a much larger role. They make bedtime more appealing to children, associating sleep with positive feelings stemming from the decorations. Its basic function is to provide a place to sleep, meaning that decorations are not necessary for it to fulfill its practical purpose. However, they change how the child views and interacts with the bed, giving the object an influence that goes beyond its original function. No one believes the bed has consciousness, yet it affects those who possess it. Agency stems not from the object itself, but from the relationship between said object and the person. The bed does not independently decide to influence the child. Instead, its decorations gain meaning through the child's own perception. An object gains agency through its interaction with living things. The bed is able to influence the child's conscious experience, showing that consciousness is not the only source of agency.

Later, Gell goes on to analyze the effect of a bamboo lime container. He says “any bamboo tube, or container of suitable size and shape, could do duty as a lime container…a plain container would hardly be as functional” (74). Gell claims that the decorated container tells much more about the “owner's” identity, serving as a sort of reflection. A relationship is created between the lime container and its owner, as the lime container becomes a representation of its owner's identity. The container's agency does not come from having a mind of its own, but from its ability to affect a social interaction, and shape another person's perception. The object and the people interacting with it work together to create meaning, making it difficult to determine how much influence comes from human consciousness versus the object itself.

The decorations on both these objects give additional meaning, and affect those who interact with them. Gell reveals that while he treats objects as non-conscious, those who encounter them are. The relationships built between objects and humans result in a sense of individual agency. Humans interact with these objects, and in turn, give meaning to them. They affect behavior, perception, and decision making. This conclusion raises the question of the role of consciousness in agency. If non living things are able to play a large role in the lives of those who hold a consciousness, what is the true role of consciousness? Gell does not directly answer this question, but his examples complicate the mindset that consciousness is a necessary part of every meaningful interaction. More directly, he points to objects as being heavily influential in these meaningful interactions. At the same time, it is possible to question whether objects possess agency, or whether humans simply give them meaning through their interactions with them. Gell's examples leave this distinction open, showing that non-conscious objects can still become part of human actions and social interactions. However, this conclusion does not answer the question on the true role of consciousness in the physical world, and its ability to create a new causal chain of its own.
