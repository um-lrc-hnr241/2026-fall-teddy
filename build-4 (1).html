Rebecca

Gell’s account of art points towards a form of reconsolidation that takes place partly outside the mind: through the “unfinished business” of patterned objects, the past remains open and is carried into the present, where identity continues to evolve through relationships with the material world. But one might object that reconsolidation does not fit Gell's conception of consciousness, as unfinished business seems to create fixity rather than revision. Reconsolidation is an idea dependent on change: the past travels into the present, where it is reshaped and goes on to affect the future. Gell's unfinished business, by contrast, is dependent on nothing ever being completed, and he describes patterns in terms of entanglement or being stuck. This relationship between person and pattern may turn out to be an ongoing loop, where the pattern and human being cannot break free. Be that as it may, I would argue that unfinished business is what keeps that loop open. Because the relationship never closes, the past remains exposed to new encounters, and each encounter can change how earlier ones are understood. Whether it leads to paralysis or revision depends on what the mind brings to it, which suggests that reconsolidation is shared between the person and the object, rather than belonging to either one alone.

To answer this objection, it helps to clarify what reconsolidation actually requires. It is not that a relationship needs to be completed, but instead, just reopened. Unfinished business keeps the past open, and each return can change it. Take for example, the carpet in Gell’s reading. Gell describes the interaction between the carpet and its owner as "inexhaustible" (80) claiming it continues on for life. Although no owner can claim to have “entirely come to grips with its pattern,” the eye “singles out now this relation, this symmetry, now that” (80). One may wonder if noticing something new is the same as revising what came before. New perceptions can be seen as additions, rather than a change to previous interpretations. However, Gell's account of complex patterns suggests that new perceptions do change what had been seen before. The viewer of a pattern picks out a single shape, but then becomes “dimly aware that this ‘ground’ on which the shape is laid has the same shapes inscribed on it, but in reverse colour" (80). What the viewer first thought they had come to terms with changes, as they look into the pattern further. Each return to the carpet causes the owner to discover something new. Gell describes this exchange as a lag that produces not a frozen balance but “some renewed, residual, imbalance” (81). The relationship never ends, but is continuously reopened and continues to change. Closure produces fixity, while unfinished business sustains continuity.

The example of apotropaic work meant to ward off a sort of evil demon seems to offer the strongest point for this objection. The demon confronting Celtic knotwork suffers a “paralysis of the will” (84) becoming stuck in the pattern. This may suggest that unfinished business leaves its subject fixed in place. What this point is missing is the goal that paralysis accomplishes. “Losing interest in whatever malevolent plan it had entertained previously” (84), the demon is not simply frozen; its past intention is displaced. What the pattern neutralizes is “the impetus of its malevolence” (86), a purpose the demon carried with it from the past. In this sense, the encounter with the pattern works like reconsolidation: the demon's past purpose is reopened in the present and does not come out the same. The trap, in other words, works by rewriting what it captures. The patterns themselves are not static either. The kolam threshold designs are rubbed out by passing feet and "renewed the next day” (85), suggesting that their protection depends on renewal rather than permanence. Even when Gell’s patterns appear to produce paralysis, they remain part of a continuous process in which the past is reopened and revised.

This raises a question about the difference between the purposes of the pattern. If the same pattern can trap one mind and change another, what makes the difference? Gell’s discussion of kolam-based tattoos in India offers an answer. He explains that the tattoo serves as both a “map” (90), leading a dead woman to a loved one, and a “puzzle” (90) aiming to protect against punishments in the land of the dead. Therefore, the object's effect depends on who encounters it. The maze tattoo serves as a guide for women towards the land of the dead, where it is put back into use. By contrast, the same design also serves as a trap, for those who meet it that have no prior relationship with the pattern. As a result, unfinished business splits into two directions. The demons are held in place, while the woman uses the pattern to carry her forward. What separates paralysis from revision is not the pattern itself but the history between the pattern and the mind that meets it.

At first glance, Gell’s unfinished business may seem disconnected from reconsolidation, since his pattern so often holds the mind in place. Yet, unfinished business is not the opposite of reconsolidation but its condition: because the relationship never closes, the past remains available to be reopened. Whether that reopening leads to paralysis or revision depends on what the mind brings to the encounter. The tattooed woman brings a lifetime with her design, so the pattern builds on her past. The demon, however, brings a malevolent plan, which the pattern overwrites. Even paralysis, then, is a kind of revision, one imposed on the mind rather than built from its own history. This suggests that reconsolidation, for Gell, is distributed between person and object, a view consistent with Gell's definition of the distributed person. Fixity, then, may not be the opposite of reconsolidation but one of its forms: when a mind meets a pattern with which it has no past. However, if one considers how Gell uses reconsolidation in order to explain why the same pattern can trap one mind but guide another, one can begin to see how reconsolidation might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course.

Build 4

In Alfred Gell’s Art and Agency, the relationship between artistic intention and an artwork’s influence raises questions about how agency can continue beyond an artist’s physical presence. I have claimed that narration connects an artist’s original intention to effects that continue after their death, suggesting that the sequence an intention sets in motion might be part of consciousness itself. By narration, I mean the continuation of an original intention through events and relationships, in which the initial intention shapes what follows without determining every outcome. However, one might object that this confuses the continuation of an intention’s effects with the continuation of consciousness itself. Gell distinguishes the mental act of creating from the physical object that remains after it. An artwork’s continuous influence demonstrates only the persistence of its effects, not of the consciousness behind it. This raises a deeper question: if consciousness ends with the artist, can their agency still remain active in the world through the artwork?

The distinction between an artist’s thoughts and their artwork initially seems to support this objection. Gell writes that “aesthetic judgements are only interior mental acts; art objects, on the other hand, are produced and circulated in the external physical and social world” (Gell 4). Yet this separation does not mean an artist’s intention becomes irrelevant once it takes physical form. Although the original mental act no longer exists, the artwork retains a structure shaped by the artist’s decisions. Their influence may therefore operate through the object they leave behind rather than through their continued consciousness. Gell’s account of artistic agency helps explain how this influence continues. He argues that “art objects are not ‘self-sufficient’ agents, but only ‘secondary’ agents in conjunction with certain specific (human) associates” (Gell 17). An artwork’s agency develops through interactions with people, allowing its effects to continue long after its creation. This complicates the relationship between an artist’s intention and an artwork’s later effects: the artist does not have to remain present for the object to shape how others interact with it. Gell explains that people readily attribute “motion and mutual causal interaction to the barest of stimuli” (Gell 78), suggesting that viewers perceive relationships and agency in what they encounter. He also describes decorative art as tending toward “the complex, the ambiguous, and the multitudinous” (Gell 79). Because viewers can respond differently to the same artwork, an artist may initiate a sequence without controlling everything that follows. The artist establishes the conditions for future interactions but does not determine their outcomes. Their agency therefore continues through relationships they initiated but can no longer direct.

This continuous influence becomes especially apparent in Gell’s discussion of decorative patterns. He describes viewers as becoming “hooked” by a pattern, which “causes us to relate in a certain way to the artefact which the pattern embellishes” (Gell 80). By calling the pattern a “mind-trap,” Gell emphasizes how its complexity draws viewers in and shapes their responses. He also notes that patterns retain their “essential ‘texture’” of hierarchies and motifs (Gell 79). Yet different viewers may experience the same pattern differently, meaning its effects are not entirely determined by the artist. The artist’s intention is therefore neither fully preserved nor entirely lost; it remains connected to the artwork’s structure while each encounter produces a potentially different response. The artwork becomes a point where an original intention meets interpretations that the artist could never have anticipated.

Still, the persistence of an artwork’s influence does not mean consciousness itself continues. An intention can produce effects that last without the original conscious act continuing. This distinction requires me to reconsider my original claim: rather than suggesting that consciousness itself survives through an artwork, I now understand narration as the process through which an original intention continues to influence events after the conscious act that produced it has ended. This changes my understanding of agency because an artist’s influence does not depend on the continued existence of their consciousness, but on the relationships their intention makes possible. Narration is therefore more than a chain of causes; it connects later events to an original intention without requiring that intention to remain mentally present. The artist’s consciousness may end, but the direction established by that consciousness can continue to shape what happens next.

Gell’s account of agency therefore helps me refine my original claim rather than abandon it. An artist’s agency may outlast their physical presence. If artwork acts as secondary agents through relationships with human associates, an artist’s original intention can influence the stream of physical causality through the object they create. The artwork establishes a direction that shapes future interactions, even when those interactions develop beyond the artist’s control. Narration does not prove that consciousness survives death; instead, it reveals how consciousness can establish sequences of events and relationships that continue after the original mental act has ended. What persists is neither the artist’s mind nor an unchanged intention, but a purposive sequence that acquires new meanings through those who encounter the artwork. Consciousness and its consequences are not identical, but narration reveals how closely they remain connected.

While I make the claim that Gell’s account of decorated objects influences the “feeling of being me”, so it cannot be located solely inside the person, one might object that influence only makes objects causes of the feeling, not parts of it. A person could be deeply shaped by their environment, but the feeling of “being me” remains wholly inside them. This objection could be supported by moments in the reading where Gell talks on page 78 about how our projection of agency onto objects causes their animation. They could also go further as Gell specifically calls decoration “psychological technology”(74), rather than a more influential action that allows us to experience the feeling of being conscious.

The objection does correctly cite where Gell shows animation begins but it fails to mention the experience is linked with that animation. During that same passage on page 78, Gell explicitly explains how the animated movement of these patterns is “not a purely subjective phenomenon, like a dream.”(78) These patterns have the ability to push back against the viewer. With complex patterns like the kolam, Gell in fact argues that when we cannot follow them we experience “pleasurable frustration”(80) over our disappointed, yet still hooked viewership of them. The exerted agency onto this object gives us back a reciprocated feeling that contributes to this phenomenality. With the kolam, even if someone knows the geometrical design, it cannot be deciphered. This suggests that if the viewer were the sole author of experience, that knowledge would let them dissolve it. The object's structure sets the specific terms of the experience, not just the author.

Even if the objector grants that the pattern pushes back, they may retreat to Gell’s description of decoration as simple “psychological technology”(74) to try and take away from my claim about decorations' deeper effects. However, this assumes technology only runs one direction. While Gell does call it a technology, it doesn’t take away from the fact that we get much more back from an object than what we give. Gell defines this relationship by saying that a decorated object “is never fully possessed at all, but is always in the process of becoming possessed,”(81) producing “an unfinished exchange”(81) between object and human. Our exchange is an ongoing process that structures our experience of interaction. Gell’s description of “technology” is not used to diminish the agency of an object but as a way to describe the reciprocity we get from complex things.

Reciprocity also undermines the assumption that objects cannot be both a technology and uniquely part of our consciousness, and Gell uses a lime-container to show why. He describes a decorated Iatmul lime-container as “less a possession than a prosthesis, a bodily organ acquired via manufacture and exchange rather than by biological growth” (74). A prosthesis is a technology, yet we also count it as part of the body that wears it. The same logic can be applied to an object, making objects something that can become part of a person.

The objection that influence is not the constitution of our consciousness is a fair challenge but it assumes a concrete boundary between the feeling of being me and what shapes it, which is something Gell doesn’t provide. Gell blends the lines of inner vs outer in his final paragraph stating that “the contrast of ‘inner’ vs ‘outer’ is always only relative rather than an absolute difference.”(222) If the line between the inner and outer is relative, then the feeling of being me need not stay on one side of it. The experience I have described is not an inner self that outside objects happen to bump into. It arises in an exchange between person and pattern, where each contributes and each limits the other, so it cannot be located only inside the person.

I began by claiming that decorated objects influence the feeling of being me, but the objection pushed me to a stronger position. The conscious feeling is partly made in the exchange rather than merely shaped by it. If phenomenality is the integrated “what it is like” of being me, then Gell suggests part of that integration happens in the encounter between person and object, where the boundary between inner and outer is only relative.

Build Four: Objection to Reflexivity

Alfred Gell’s concept of agency in Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory leads to an understanding of how objects have the ability to influence people, whether they realize it or not. Once people are aware of their awareness of, say, a challenge presented by a pattern, Gell’s framework identifies the process as reflexivity. One possible objection to this notion is that the self-awareness seen in reflexivity does not necessarily need to stem from a defined moment of crisis as one typically might understand a crisis to be (a huge event or disaster). Critics might argue that a person who encounters the same pattern they previously understood or became aware of can draw on their last encounter to interpret/view it again. Their perspective might be that this is not exactly a moment of crisis because the pattern is recurring to the individual. Nonetheless, this objection fails to account for the reflection and thought that must go into reflexivity. Gell emphasizes that after one is aware of a challenge in a pattern, one must retain some knowledge in order to act on it. Otherwise, they are simply left aware of the challenge’s existence without having the opportunity to take action.

The objection of reflexivity stemming from a recurring, completed challenge can be formulated from Gell’s discussion of how Malakulans saw patterns not as “independent visual objects at all, but as performances, like dances, in which men could reveal their capability” (Gell 94). They repeatedly created patterns, which elicited the idea that they were used to the method of sharing their knowledge and, therefore, were not placed in a moment of crisis as they were observing their work. The objection would argue that they automatically expressed reflexivity due to the recurrence. However, the drawback to this example is that they did not necessarily use the same exact patterns each time. While yes, they needed some form of knowledge to solve the challenges, they may likely be actively reflecting on their understanding, which is what produces reflexivity. Furthermore, the person also may have changed between the encounters; they might be aware that they have solved or encountered the pattern and may come at it from a new lens, which can also trigger reflexivity.

A better example from Gell that argues against the objection of automatic reflexivity is kolam, which involves powdered patterns created by women to ensnare demons and prevent them from crossing house thresholds (Gell 85). With kolam, the demon pondering the pattern is unable to comprehend it, but the women creating it do. By the objection’s logic, the women, as they draw out the designs, always exhibit reflexivity simply because they were aware of the designs the first time, so they must be aware each subsequent time. Cultural knowledge would support the objection in the sense that the women know that the patterns are created for protection— they are aware of the pattern’s purpose. However, that only composes one part of the awareness of reflexivity; the other part requires an understanding or investigation of the pattern being created. The kolam “seems to consist of a single line…However…is actually composed of four continuous loops of asymmetric configuration” (Gell 85). If the women consider the formation of the multiple loops each time that they place the powder and recognize their questioning or comprehension, their activity can potentially induce reflexivity. If they only copy the structure, they are not actively questioning or understanding the challenge at hand, failing to establish reflexivity.

Gell began his introduction to patterns with the description of a child being inclined to sleep in a bed covered with jolly patterns. Reflexivity was not used because the child did not ponder what about the pattern prompted them to sleep (Gell 74). In contrast, the Malakulans and kolam did actively reflect on their patterns’ purposes and visuals. When there is an understanding or interrogation of a pattern, regardless of whether in a time of crisis, reflexivity is possible. When there is simply mindless recreation or no curiosity about why one is repeating an action done previously or what might be different about their action, a person is only observing their surroundings, not necessarily being aware of how they are actualized through their relationship with them.

Emily

In my first reading of Gell, I focused on how complex patterns capture attention and create what he calls a “paralysis of the will.” I initially understood this as viewers becoming trapped in the attempt to mentally reconstruct a pattern they cannot fully grasp. After rereading Gell, however, I became more interested in what happens to consciousness during that experience. I argued in Build 3 that complex patterns can provoke reflexivity, or awareness that one is aware, by interrupting perception and making viewers notice mental processes they normally overlook. However, I now think this relationship is more complicated. While difficulty can create an opportunity for reflexivity, becoming absorbed in a pattern does not necessarily mean becoming aware of one's own consciousness.

Gell begins to reveal this relationship when he describes “‘seeing’” simple geometric figures as “mentally intending or projecting the construction of the figure, line by line” (85). His use of “intending” suggests that perception involves mental activity rather than simply receiving an image. With simple figures, this process happens so effortlessly that we rarely notice it. Complex patterns, however, can interrupt this process. Gell's discussion of the kolam demonstrates this difficulty. He “‘knows’ (intellectually)” that the design consists of “four separate, but identical loops differently orientated,” yet finds it “extremely frustrating to attempt to abstract individual loops from the overall design” (85). Although he understands how the pattern is constructed, he cannot make his perception follow that knowledge. This difference between knowing and seeing suggests that reflexivity becomes possible when someone recognizes the limitations of their own perception.

One might object, however, that Gell's patterns actually prevent reflexivity rather than encourage it. His idea of the “paralysis of the will” describes how complex patterns can capture someone's attention and frustrate their attempts to understand or move beyond them. If someone becomes completely absorbed in reconstructing a pattern, would they necessarily have the awareness needed to reflect on their own consciousness? Gell's discussion of the maze strengthens this objection. He explains that without “knowledge of the completed geometrical figure,” the dead person “will never find the road” and “never reaches the land of the dead” (93). Although the pattern interrupts the dead person's intended journey, Gell never establishes that this interruption produces self-awareness. The dead person may simply remain trapped, focused entirely on finding a way through. This challenges my original interpretation because experiencing an obstacle does not necessarily mean recognizing how one's own consciousness responds to it.

However, Gell's experience with the kolam reveals something that this objection overlooks. Unlike the dead person in the maze, Gell explicitly recognizes the difference between what he intellectually understands and what he can actually perceive. His frustration matters because he becomes aware that his perception is not responding to his intentions. Rather than simply struggling with the pattern, he recognizes the struggle as part of his own experience. This is what makes his experience reflexive: his attention extends beyond the object toward his awareness of perceiving it. Looking back at my Build 3 argument, I think I assumed too quickly that interrupted intentions naturally produce reflexivity. The maze demonstrates how patterns can influence action without necessarily producing self-awareness, while the kolam suggests that reflexivity becomes possible when someone recognizes the gap between intention and experience. This recognition might also arise through surprise or curiosity rather than frustration alone.

This distinction changes how I understand the relationship between reflexivity and control. Initially, I emphasized that becoming aware of our mental processes does not necessarily give us control over them. Gell cannot simply force himself to perceive the individual loops despite understanding their arrangement. However, perhaps reflexivity does not require complete control to influence what someone does next. Recognizing a perceptual limitation might encourage someone to reconsider their approach, redirect their attention, or deliberately try another way of understanding the pattern. Although Gell does not demonstrate that reflexivity necessarily produces these changes, his discussion raises the possibility that awareness can influence our responses even when perception itself remains difficult to control.

If we consider how Gell's patterns can provoke reflexivity by revealing the difference between intention and perception, we can begin to see how reflexivity might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality. Rather than simply observing an automatic response, someone who recognizes that response might deliberately alter their subsequent actions. This does not mean consciousness can overcome every limitation, but it suggests that reflexivity may matter because recognizing how we experience something can influence how we choose to respond to it.
