Build Three: Reflexivity

My initial interpretation of Gell’s Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory focused on how agency belonged specifically to the person acting on an object. However, the writing explains that agency can extend to an object despite it not necessarily having consciousness. According to Gell, agency lies in a network of social connections, and objects are a part of this network. Objects can invoke emotions in a person, distributing thoughts and intentions. As this process of agency occurs, the body can find itself in a strange position: acting with reflexivity. Reflexivity is defined as awareness that one is aware of something different or of one's environment. It occurs during moments of stress, crisis, or perceived threat when an intentional decision is of better use compared to a non-impulsive decision. Gell goes into depth about the role of patterns in a person’s life relating to the agency described previously, which can trigger reflexivity.

Patterns are considered objects that can be held dear to a person and can influence the person’s actions. Gell’s writing depicts a child feeling more inclined to sleep in a bed covered in a jolly pattern, such as dinosaurs (Gell 74), than one without such a pattern. Similarly, a person may prefer a cup with a specific set of shapes and colors compared to a plain one. Here, the pattern acts by causing a person to admire it and hold it more dearly to them, displaying agency through the object rather than only through the person whose agency allows them to observe the object. Despite influencing decisions, the responses do not necessarily require an understanding of the pattern’s significance, suggesting that the agency of an object on a person can precede their conscious reflection.

Aside from being aesthetically captivating, a pattern can represent a difficult journey filled with obstacles that must be overcome. This relates to a pattern’s intricacy and composition, for it can confuse the one who looks at it— a small moment of crisis. For example, a diagram of winding lines that overlap with one another repeatedly can be puzzling, for where does it begin and end? Completing the pattern becomes a challenge, as described for the Malakulans, in which the patterns were not seen as “independent visual objects at all, but as performances, like dances, in which men could reveal their capability” (Gell 94). Compared to children who may not understand why they are drawn to sleep, the Malakulans are aware that there is something intentional about the pattern displayed and, through their attempt at creating a solution, become aware of how they are examining it rather than just perceiving the object as a whole without looking past its appearance.

Gell further discusses the role of patterns in testing the capabilities. In Malakulan culture, on the passage to the afterlife, ghosts go along a road where a completed geometrical figure is incomplete. Seeing that, the ghost “loses his track and cannot find it. Only a knowledge of the completed geometrical figure can release him” (Gell 93). The ghost gains awareness as it learns a solution from the pattern on the ground and uses it to be released. The difference between the successful and failed ghosts lies in their ability to interpret. Recognition does not guarantee a solution. The agency of the pattern not only presents an obstacle, but also reveals the intention of the guardian ghost, which is to safeguard the afterlife. For the ghosts that may be aware there is a pattern but do not necessarily have the “knowledge” required to complete it, they are unable to act upon their reflexivity. In reality, it can lead to frustration and no remedy for the moment of crisis.

Gell’s articulation of reflexivity can be used to depict a quality of consciousness. Reflexivity does not only focus on the world around a person, but emphasizes how the person’s own knowledge and perspective shape their surroundings. The ghost problem illustrates how recognition and resolution are distinct from one another. It demonstrates that objects can contain information not easily visible, and this concept helps one to reconsider the boundaries of agency, which emerges from the connections between an object and people. Awareness, as a part of reflexivity, can help one recognize a problem, but it may not always provide a resolution, meaning the person may be aware but cannot act on it.
